Accounting Fundamentals for Funds

A fund is just a set of books where the "business" is holding investments. Everything in fund accounting reduces to double entry, applied to securities and investors.

The equation and the statements

Assets = Liabilities + Net assets (equity). In a fund, net assets belong to investors; that figure is the NAV. Net assets are broken down into each investor's capital account (partnership) or shares × NAV per share (corporation/mutual fund).

Double entry in 60 seconds

Every transaction has equal debits and credits. Debits increase assets and expenses; credits increase liabilities, equity and income. If the entry doesn't balance, something is missing — which is itself a useful control (a system that cannot post an unbalanced entry).

Worked example: buy 1,000 shares at $50, commission $25

Investments at cost50,025

Commissions on purchases normally become part of cost; on sales they reduce proceeds. Settlement day: debit the payable, credit cash.

Trade date vs settlement date

Investment companies book on trade date: the day the fund becomes economically exposed. The cash moves later, on settlement date (T+1 for most US equities since May 2024; varies by market). Between the two there's a payable or receivable. Get this wrong and your cash looks fine while your NAV is wrong.

Try it: a fund buys 500 shares at $20 on Monday and settles on Wednesday. What is on its books on Tuesday?

The position is already there: Investments 10,000 (plus any commission) and a payable for investments purchased 10,000. Cash is untouched and NAV is unchanged, because the trade is booked on trade date.

Accrual accounting

Income and expenses are recognised when earned or incurred, not when cash moves. For funds this means daily accruals of bond interest, dividend income from the ex-date (not pay date), management fees, admin fees and interest on financing.

Ex-date, record date, pay date. Dividends are recognised on the ex-dividend date. The fund records a dividend receivable; when cash arrives on the pay date the receivable clears. Short positions owe the dividend ("dividend on short") on the same ex-date.

Realized vs unrealized P&L

  • Unrealized: change in value of positions still held. Moves every time the price or FX rate moves.
  • Realized: locked in at sale or cover. Realized = proceeds − cost (of the lots relieved). When a position is sold, the previously recorded unrealized gain is reversed and realized gain recorded, so total P&L doesn't double count.

Total period P&L is the same either way; what changes is classification — and that matters for tax and for reporting realized vs unrealized.

Cost basis and lot relief

When you sell part of a position bought in several lots you must choose which lots are relieved. Methods: FIFO, LIFO, HIFO (highest cost first), average cost, or specific lot. The method set in the fund's policy drives realized P&L and tax character. Always confirm the method before debugging a "wrong" gain.

Foreign currency

A fund has a base (functional) currency. Positions are held in local currency and translated at the daily FX rate. For investment funds, FX effects on holdings are often reported as part of the net realized/unrealized gain on investments; separating "market" from "currency" gain is a reporting choice (ASC 946 allows combining).
