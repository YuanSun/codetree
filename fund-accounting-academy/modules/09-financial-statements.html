Financial Statements & Audit

Annual financial statements are the fund's formal record. Funds follow investment company accounting (US GAAP ASC 946 or IFRS with IFRS 10 investment-entity rules).

Statement set

StatementWhat it shows
Statement of Assets and Liabilities (US) / Statement of Financial Position (IFRS)Investments at fair value, cash, receivables, payables, net assets, NAV per share
Schedule of InvestmentsEvery holding (or those above 5% of NAV with others aggregated) with fair value, % of NAV, country/industry; open derivatives; securities sold short
Statement of OperationsInvestment income, expenses, realised and unrealised gains
Statement of Changes in Net AssetsOperations plus capital activity (subs/reds, distributions)
Statement of Cash FlowsMay be exempt for investment companies meeting ASC 230 conditions (e.g. substantially all assets are Level 1/2, little debt)
Financial HighlightsTotal return, expense and net investment income ratios, per-share data, by class or investor group
NotesAccounting policies, fair value levels, derivatives, related parties, fees, risks, subsequent events

Ratios in financial highlights

  • Total return: time-weighted, computed on a class/investor basis, before/after incentive fee, not annualised for periods under a year.
  • Expense ratio: expenses ÷ average net assets (before and after incentive fee shown separately).
  • Net investment income ratio: net investment income ÷ average net assets.
  • Portfolio turnover: required for mutual funds.

Reporting derivatives

Disclose notional, fair value by contract type and location in the statements, and the effect on operations (ASC 815 / IFRS 7). Offsetting of derivative assets and liabilities needs a legal right of offset (master netting arrangements) and an accounting policy election.

The annual audit process

  1. Planning: auditor sets scope and materiality, identifies risk areas (valuation of Level 3, completeness of liabilities).
  2. PBC list: trial balance, subledger, broker confirmations, bank statements, pricing evidence, investor capital register, fee calculations, board minutes.
  3. Fieldwork: auditor independently confirms with brokers/custodians and counterparties; tests valuations; re-performs fee calculations.
  4. Draft statements: administrator drafts; manager and board review; auditor issues the opinion.
  5. Subsequent events: events after year-end (large redemptions, valuation changes) may need disclosure.
Before the audit starts Clear aged breaks, tie capital accounts to NAV, and have each estimate documented. Tidy books make the audit cheaper — and funds care.

Tax basics a fund accountant should recognise

  • US partnerships: allocate taxable income to partners; K-1s; Section 704(c); wash sale and straddle rules; partnership-level 475(f) elections for traders.
  • Offshore funds: usually tax-transparent for corporate tax but investors may face PFIC/CFC regimes.
  • Withholding on dividends/interest and tax reclaim processes.
  • Book-to-tax differences: unrealised gains, organizational costs, wash sales.
Tax is jurisdiction-specific. Treat these as vocabulary, not advice, and defer to your firm's tax team for treatment.
