Investor Accounting

The portfolio side tells you what the fund is worth. The investor side tells you who owns what. This is where most senior-level complexity lives.

The sum of all investors' closing capital must equal fund NAV. That tie-out is the single most important check on the investor side.

Try it: fund NAV is 5,000,000 but the investors' closing balances add up to 4,987,500. Where do you look?

A 12,500 gap. Check, in order: a late subscription or redemption not in the capital accounts; a fee or expense accrued in the portfolio books but not allocated; an allocation run on stale data before a late trade was fixed; then rounding across many investors.

Allocating P&L

Common approaches:

  • Pro-rata by beginning capital (with a capital-weighted adjustment when money moves mid-period).
  • Daily/valuation-date allocation: P&L each dealing date goes to capital at that time. Cleanest when flows occur intra-month.
  • Special allocations: expenses charged only to a class; hedged-class FX P&L; side-pocket P&L to participating investors only.

Use the allocation calculator to see how weighting changes who gets what.

Subscriptions and redemptions

  • Dealing day: the NAV on which units are struck. Subscription money is typically received before (cash in a "subscriptions received in advance" liability) and units issued at the dealing NAV.
  • Notice periods and lock-ups: redemption requests (e.g. 45 days' notice, quarterly) are recorded as redemptions payable liabilities from the dealing day, reducing NAV even though cash leaves later.
  • Gates: the fund limits total redemptions in a period (e.g. 20% of NAV); excess is carried forward pro-rata.
  • Holdbacks: e.g. 5–10% of a full redemption withheld until audit.

Why equalization or series exist

If a fund charges a performance fee on fund performance, an investor joining after a good month would either pay a fee on gains made before they joined, or the older investors would be undercharged. Two fixes:

Series accounting

Each subscription month is a separate series with its own NAV and HWM. Gains are tracked per series; periodically (e.g. annually) series with positive performance roll up to a lead series. Fair, but more share classes to manage.

Equalization (equalisation factor / credit)

Everyone is in one class. A late entrant who subscribes at a NAV already net of an accrued performance fee pays an equalization credit (or buys extra shares) to compensate; if the NAV is below the HWM they pay a depreciation deposit. The goal is that every investor's performance fee equals what they'd pay for their own return.

Key idea. The fund can only have one NAV per share per class. Everything else (series, equalization) is machinery that lets each investor still pay a fee that reflects their own gain.

Side pockets and designated investments

An illiquid position is moved to a separate class; investors at that time receive a proportional special share class. New subscribers don't share. Redeemers get their main-class balance but keep the side pocket until realization. Fees often follow different rules: usually charged only on realised gains.

Transfers, tax and withholding

  • Transfers between investors need legal approval and carry cost basis and HWM.
  • US partnerships issue K-1s (tax allocations differ from book allocations — "book/tax differences").
  • Withholding on non-US investors is handled separately from NAV accounting.

Controls specific to investor accounting

  • Capital accounts sum to NAV every dealing date.
  • Transfer-agent register ties to share counts.
  • Subscriptions agree to bank statements (don't issue units for unreceived cash without approval).
  • Redemptions checked to documents, notice dates, lock-ups, gates, KYC/AML clearance.
