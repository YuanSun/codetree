The Fund Industry & Structures

Before you can account for a fund you need to know who else touches its money and data. Most "fund accounting problems" are really handoff problems between these parties.

The cast of characters

PartyWhat they doWhy you care
Investor (LP / shareholder)Puts money in and owns a share of the fund.Their capital account or share balance is what you ultimately report.
General Partner (GP) / BoardLegally controls the fund and appoints service providers. In a hedge fund the GP is often an affiliate of the manager.Signs off financials and policy decisions (e.g. valuation policy).
Investment manager (IM)Makes the investment decisions; earns management and performance fees.Source of trades, pricing views, and most urgent questions.
Fund administratorIndependent party that keeps the books, strikes the NAV, processes investor dealing, produces statements. This is usually you.Independence from the manager is the whole value proposition.
CustodianSafekeeps assets (typically for long-only / regulated funds). Holds title.Source of position and cash statements to reconcile to.
Prime broker (PB)Executes/clears, finances leverage, lends stock for shorts, holds collateral for hedge funds.Daily statements; margin, financing and borrow charges.
AuditorGives an opinion on annual financial statements.Your records must support every number; expect PBC (provided-by-client) lists.
Transfer agentMaintains the shareholder register (common for mutual funds and ETFs).Share counts must agree to the fund's books.
Legal counsel / tax advisorOffering documents, side letters, tax reporting (K-1, PFIC, etc.).Define the rules your system has to implement.

Fund types and what changes in the accounting

TypeTypical featuresAccounting emphasis
Hedge fundLong/short, derivatives, leverage; monthly (sometimes weekly/daily) dealing; partnership or corporation.Daily P&L, derivatives, financing, investor-level fees (HWM, equalization, series).
Mutual fund / UCITS / ETFOpen-ended, daily NAV, regulated, strict diversification rules.Daily NAV accuracy, shares and NAV per share, distributions, regulatory ratios.
Private equity / ventureCapital calls and distributions over 8–12 years; illiquid holdings.Commitments, waterfalls, fair-value marks, IRR/TVPI.
Real estate / credit fundsLoans or property; income-driven.Interest accruals, amortization, impairments, loan servicing data.
Fund of fundsInvests in other funds.Valuation off other funds' estimated NAVs; lag between estimate and final NAV.

Common structures

Master–feeder

Onshore (US taxable) and offshore (non-US / tax-exempt) investors each invest in their own feeder; both feeders invest in a single master that holds the portfolio. The master's P&L is allocated to feeders by their share of master capital; each feeder then allocates to its own investors and charges its own fees.

Series and classes

One legal fund can offer different classes (different fee terms, currencies, or hedging) and, in some hedge funds, monthly series of shares so each subscription tracks its own performance-fee base. We cover this properly in Module 06.

Side pocket / SPV

Illiquid or hard-to-value investments can be segregated so redeeming investors don't take a mark they can't realise. Only investors who held at designation participate.

Dealing frequency drives workload. A daily-NAV mutual fund can't rely on month-end clean-up; a monthly hedge fund can estimate daily but must be exactly right on the dealing day. Always ask: "When is the NAV used to transact?"

What drives the cost of running a fund

Funds are priced on asset-based fees to the administrator (basis points on NAV) and often minimums per share class or investor. Complex structures (many series, side pockets, FX-hedged classes) cost more to run — useful context when someone asks "why does this take so long?".
