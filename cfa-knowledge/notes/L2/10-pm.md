## What moves asset prices: cash flows, rates and risk premiums
@id economics-and-markets
@r 34
@aka economic factors and asset prices; discount rate components; real risk free rate; inflation premium; risk premium; expectations and valuation; surprises move prices
@see real-vs-nominal-rates; risk-free-rate; business-cycle-assets

Any asset's value is the PV of its expected cash flows, so economic news can only move prices by changing: (1) **expected cash flows**, (2) **default-free real interest rates**, (3) **expected inflation**, or (4) **risk premiums**. Only **changes in expectations** (surprises) move prices; well-anticipated news is already priced in.

## Real rates, growth and consumption smoothing
@id real-rates-growth
@r 34
@aka real interest rate and economic growth; consumption smoothing; intertemporal rate of substitution; real risk free rate level; volatility of GDP growth and real rates
@see economics-and-markets; potential-gdp

Investors would rather smooth consumption over time. When the economy is expected to grow faster, people want to borrow against higher future income, so **real interest rates are higher**. When growth is more **volatile** (more uncertainty), people save more for safety, pushing real rates lower... though they also demand higher risk premiums on risky assets. The real default-free rate is thus linked to the trend rate and the volatility of economic growth.

## Business cycle and short-term rates, curve slope, bonds
@id business-cycle-assets
@r 34
@aka business cycle and yield curve; Taylor rule; policy rate and output gap; yield curve slope recession; government bonds as hedge; inflation-indexed bonds breakeven; credit spreads over cycle
@see business-cycle; yield-curve-shapes; monetary-policy

- **Short-term rates** follow policy. The **Taylor rule**: policy rate = neutral rate + expected inflation + 0.5(inflation gap) + 0.5(output gap).
- **Yield curve slope**: steep in early recovery (low short rates, expected rises); flat or inverted near peaks (tight policy). An inverted curve has historically preceded recessions.
- **Default-free bonds** do best when growth and inflation surprise downward; they hedge bad economic times (negative correlation with equities when inflation is stable).
- **Inflation-indexed bonds**: the **breakeven inflation rate** (nominal − real yield) includes expected inflation plus an inflation risk premium, and is affected by liquidity differences.

## Credit spreads and the cycle; product markets and credit
@id credit-cycle-assets
@r 34
@aka credit spreads business cycle; credit sensitive bonds performance; company credit quality product market; spread widening recession; high yield vs investment grade over cycle
@see yield-spread-drivers; credit-cycle

Credit spreads **widen** in downturns (higher expected defaults and higher risk premiums) and **narrow** in expansions; lower-rated bonds move most. Credit-sensitive bonds therefore underperform governments in recessions. A company's credit quality depends on its product market: cyclical demand, high operating leverage and weak pricing power make cash flows (and credit) more vulnerable in downturns.

## Equity risk premium, earnings growth and valuation multiples over the cycle
@id equity-cycle
@r 34
@aka equity risk premium consumption hedging; equities bad times; earnings growth over cycle; cyclical vs defensive earnings; PE multiples over cycle; commercial real estate economic factors
@see equity-risk-premium; business-cycle

Equities tend to do badly in **bad economic times**, when investors most need money: they are poor consumption hedges, so investors demand a **positive and relatively large equity risk premium**. The ERP rises in recessions.

**Earnings growth** is most volatile for cyclical companies; short-term expectations swing with the cycle, long-term expectations less so. **Valuation multiples** tend to be high when growth is expected to be strong and risk premiums are low (often early in recoveries), and compress when risk premiums rise. **Commercial real estate** behaves like a mix of bonds (lease income) and equities (economic sensitivity and refinancing risk), and is hurt in downturns.

## Value added and the information ratio
@id active-return-ir
@r 35
@aka value added active management; active return; active weights; information ratio ex ante; information ratio ex post; IR vs Sharpe ratio; optimal active risk; combining active portfolio with benchmark
@see information-ratio; fundamental-law; sharpe-ratio

**Value added (active return)** = portfolio return − benchmark return = Σ(active weight × asset return). **Active risk** = tracking error.

The **information ratio** \(IR = (R_p - R_B)/\sigma(R_p - R_B)\) measures active return per unit of active risk; it's unaffected by adding cash or leverage to the active positions. The best actively managed portfolio has the highest IR. Optimal active risk: \(\sigma_A^* = \frac{IR}{SR_B}\sigma_B\), and the resulting Sharpe ratio is \(SR_P^2 = SR_B^2 + IR^2\). So when choosing managers, **pick the highest IR**, then set the amount of active risk.

## Fundamental law of active management
@id fundamental-law
@r 35
@aka fundamental law of active management; information coefficient; IC; breadth; BR; transfer coefficient; TC; Grinold; IR equals IC times square root of breadth
@see active-return-ir

$$E(R_A) = TC \times IC \times \sqrt{BR}\times\sigma_A \qquad IR \approx TC \times IC \times \sqrt{BR}$$

- **Information coefficient (IC)**: correlation between forecast and actual active returns (skill).
- **Breadth (BR)**: number of **independent** active decisions per year.
- **Transfer coefficient (TC)**: how well forecasts translate into portfolio weights (constraints like long-only reduce it).

Implications: you can raise IR by being more skilful, by making more independent bets, or by loosening constraints. **Market timing** has low breadth (few independent decisions per year) and needs high skill; **security selection** across many stocks can succeed with a small IC. Limits: decisions are rarely independent, and IC is hard to estimate.

## ETF creation/redemption and authorized participants
@id etf-creation-redemption
@r 36
@aka ETF creation redemption; authorized participant; AP; creation basket; creation unit; in kind redemption; ETF arbitrage mechanism; ETF tax efficiency
@see pooled-investments; etf-premium-discount

**Authorized participants** (large dealers) create ETF shares by delivering a **creation basket** of securities (or cash) to the fund in exchange for a block of ETF shares (a creation unit), and redeem by doing the reverse. If the ETF trades **above** NAV, APs create shares and sell them; if **below**, they buy shares and redeem them. This arbitrage keeps prices close to NAV. **In-kind** redemptions let the fund dispose of low-cost-basis securities without realizing gains, making ETFs tax-efficient.

## ETF trading, spreads, premiums and discounts
@id etf-premium-discount
@r 36
@aka ETF bid ask spread; ETF premium to NAV; ETF discount to NAV; intraday NAV; iNAV; stale prices; ETF liquidity; secondary market trading ETF
@see etf-creation-redemption; etf-risks-costs

Most ETF trading happens between investors on exchanges; only net imbalances trigger creations or redemptions. **Bid-ask spreads** reflect the cost of creating/redeeming, hedging and trading the underlying basket, the underlying's own spreads, and the AP's risk and profit. Spreads are tight for ETFs on liquid large-cap stocks, wider for less liquid underlyings (high yield, emerging markets).

**Premiums and discounts** arise from timing differences (underlying markets closed, e.g. foreign stocks), **stale or illiquid** underlying prices (the NAV may be the stale number, not the ETF price), and market stress when APs step back.

## ETF tracking error, costs and risks
@id etf-risks-costs
@r 36
@aka ETF tracking error; tracking difference; ETF costs; expense ratio; trading costs ETF; ETF risks; counterparty risk ETF; synthetic ETF; leveraged ETF; inverse ETF; ETN; settlement risk; closures
@see etf-premium-discount; tracking-error

**Tracking error sources**: fees and expenses, representative sampling instead of full replication, index changes and rebalancing, cash drag, dividend timing, securities lending income (helps), and withholding taxes.

**Costs of ownership**: management fee, trading costs (commissions, bid-ask spread, premiums/discounts), taxes.

**Risks**: counterparty risk (synthetic ETFs using swaps; ETNs are unsecured debt of the issuer), settlement risk, fund closure risk, **leveraged and inverse ETFs** (reset daily, so long-run returns drift from the multiple: "volatility decay"), and investor misuse.

## APT and multifactor models (Level II)
@id apt-l2
@r 37
@aka arbitrage pricing theory assumptions; APT equation; arbitrage opportunity factor portfolio; factor risk premium; pure factor portfolio; expected return factor sensitivities
@see apt; multifactor-model-types

APT assumptions: returns are described by a factor model; there are many assets so firm-specific risk can be diversified away; no arbitrage opportunities exist. Then:

$$E(R_p) = R_F + \lambda_1\beta_{p1} + \cdots + \lambda_K\beta_{pK}$$

An **arbitrage opportunity** exists if two portfolios with the same factor exposures have different expected returns: buy the higher, sell the lower, with zero net exposure. A **pure factor portfolio** has a sensitivity of 1 to one factor and 0 to the others.

## Macroeconomic, fundamental and statistical factor models
@id multifactor-model-types
@r 37
@aka macroeconomic factor model; fundamental factor model; statistical factor model; factor surprises; standardized sensitivities; Barra style models; principal components factor model
@see apt-l2; return-generating-models

- **Macroeconomic** models: factors are **surprises** in macro variables (GDP growth, inflation, credit spreads). Sensitivities estimated by time-series regression.
- **Fundamental** models: factors are attributes such as P/E, size, momentum, leverage, industry. Sensitivities are **standardized** attribute values; factor returns estimated by cross-sectional regression each period.
- **Statistical** models: factors extracted from returns (factor analysis, PCA); good fit but hard to interpret.

Benefits of multiple dimensions: identifies risks the market prices (beyond beta), helps build portfolios with targeted exposures, and explains returns in bad times.

## Active risk decomposition and factor uses
@id active-risk-decomposition
@r 37
@aka active risk decomposition; active factor risk; active specific risk; tracking risk; return attribution; risk budgeting; factor portfolio; tracking portfolio
@see tracking-error; active-return-ir

**Active risk²** = **active factor risk** (from differences between portfolio and benchmark factor exposures) + **active specific risk** (from security-specific bets). Managers can see whether their risk comes from intended stock picks or unintended factor tilts.

Uses of multifactor models: **return attribution** (how much of active return came from factors vs selection), **risk budgeting**, building **factor portfolios** (to bet on a factor) and **tracking portfolios** (to replicate a benchmark's factor exposures with fewer holdings).

## VaR methods: parametric, historical, Monte Carlo
@id var-methods
@r 38
@aka parametric VaR; variance covariance VaR; delta normal; historical simulation VaR; Monte Carlo VaR; VaR calculation; VaR time scaling; square root of time
@see var; historical-simulation; monte-carlo

$$\text{Parametric VaR} = [E(R) - z\,\sigma] \times V \qquad VaR_{T\text{ days}} \approx VaR_{1\text{ day}}\sqrt{T}$$

| Method | How | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parametric (variance-covariance) | Assumes normal returns; uses means, σ, correlations | Simple, fast | Ignores fat tails; poor for options (non-linear) |
| Historical simulation | Reprices today's portfolio with past returns; the 5th-percentile loss | No distribution assumption; handles options | Depends on the chosen past period |
| Monte Carlo | Simulates thousands of scenarios from assumed distributions | Flexible; any distribution and payoff | Model-dependent; computationally heavy |

## VaR advantages, limitations and extensions
@id var-limits
@r 38
@aka VaR advantages; VaR limitations; conditional VaR; CVaR; expected shortfall; incremental VaR; marginal VaR; relative VaR; ex ante tracking error; tail risk
@see var-methods; var

**Advantages**: one number, easy to communicate; comparable across portfolios; used for capital allocation and regulation.

**Limitations**: subjective choices (confidence, horizon, method); says nothing about losses **beyond** the threshold; underestimates the frequency of extreme events with normal assumptions; ignores liquidity; not sub-additive in some cases; a focus on the left tail only.

**Extensions**: **conditional VaR (expected shortfall)**: average loss beyond VaR; **incremental VaR**: change in VaR from adding or removing a position; **marginal VaR**: change per unit of a position; **relative VaR (ex ante tracking error)**: VaR of the difference from the benchmark.

## Sensitivity and scenario risk measures
@id sensitivity-scenario-measures
@r 38
@aka sensitivity measures; scenario analysis; stress testing; historical scenarios; hypothetical scenarios; reverse stress test; beta duration delta gamma vega exposure; convexity exposure
@see risk-measures; var-limits; option-greeks

**Sensitivity measures** show how value changes for a small change in one factor: **beta** (equity), **duration and convexity** (bonds), **delta, gamma, vega** (options). Useful but don't give probabilities.

**Scenario measures**: **historical scenarios** (replay a past crisis on today's portfolio), **hypothetical scenarios** (designed events, including **reverse stress tests** that look for scenarios that would cause unacceptable losses). They capture extreme, correlated moves that VaR misses, but scenario choice is subjective and they have no probability attached. Use them together with VaR.

## Market risk constraints and capital allocation
@id risk-constraints
@r 38
@aka risk budgeting; position limits; scenario limits; stop loss limits; risk limits; economic capital; risk measures by institution; banks vs asset managers vs pension funds vs insurers
@see var-methods; risk-management

Constraints: **risk budgets** (allocate total VaR or tracking error across desks or managers), **position limits** (caps on exposures), **scenario limits** (maximum loss in a stress scenario), **stop-loss limits** (cut positions after a loss threshold). Risk measures also guide **capital allocation**: assign capital in proportion to risk, compare return on risk capital across activities.

Who uses what: **banks** (VaR, liquidity, leverage, economic capital, regulatory capital), **asset managers** (tracking error, VaR, scenario analysis, ex post performance), **pension funds** (surplus at risk, liability-relative risk), **insurers** (sensitivity and scenario measures, solvency capital; P&C focus on catastrophe risk, life on interest rates and longevity).

## Backtesting: objectives and steps
@id backtesting
@r 39
@aka backtesting; backtest; walk forward; rolling window backtest; strategy design; investment universe; rebalancing; performance metrics backtest; backtest visuals
@see backtest-pitfalls; historical-simulation

**Backtesting** simulates how a strategy would have performed on historical data, to judge its potential before committing capital.

Steps: (1) **strategy design**: define the investment universe, signals and the rules for weights and rebalancing; (2) **historical investment simulation**: typically a **rolling window** (walk-forward) method that estimates on past data and tests on the next period, then rolls forward; (3) **analysis**: return, volatility, Sharpe ratio, drawdowns, turnover, factor exposures, skewness and kurtosis; visuals such as cumulative return charts and drawdown charts.

## Problems in backtesting
@id backtest-pitfalls
@r 39
@aka survivorship bias backtest; look ahead bias backtest; data snooping; overfitting backtest; transaction costs backtest; short selling constraints; regime changes; p hacking
@see backtesting; sampling-methods; overfitting

- **Survivorship bias**: using only companies still in existence.
- **Look-ahead bias**: using data not available at the time (point-in-time data fixes it).
- **Data snooping / overfitting**: trying many variants until one works; use out-of-sample tests, adjust significance thresholds (e.g. higher t-statistics), cross-validation.
- **Ignoring costs and constraints**: transaction costs, market impact, short-selling availability, capacity.
- **Regime change**: relationships that held in one era may fail later.

## Historical scenario analysis and simulation (Level II)
@id scenario-simulation-l2
@r 39
@aka historical scenario analysis; Monte Carlo vs historical simulation; simulation inputs; distribution assumptions; fat tails simulation; sensitivity analysis simulation; regime based scenarios
@see historical-simulation; monte-carlo; sensitivity-scenario-measures

**Historical scenario analysis** tests a strategy in specific regimes (crises, inflation spikes, recessions) to see how it behaves when it matters.

**Simulation**: **historical simulation** (resampling actual past returns, possibly with replacement) keeps real fat tails and correlations but is limited to what happened; **Monte Carlo** draws from assumed distributions (e.g. multivariate normal, or fat-tailed ones), and needs decisions on the distribution, parameters, correlations and number of trials. Run **sensitivity analysis** by changing key inputs (e.g. assuming fat tails instead of normal) to see how robust the conclusions are.
