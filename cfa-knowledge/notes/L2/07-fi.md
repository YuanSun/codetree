## Spot, forward and YTM relationships
@id spot-forward-ytm
@r 23
@aka spot rates and forward rates relationship; forward rate model; YTM as average of spot rates; expected return vs realized return; forward pricing model
@see spot-rate; forward-rate; yield-curve-strategies

- The **forward pricing model**: the price of a zero maturing at T + j, one period forward, equals its price today divided by the price of a T-year zero: \(F(T, T+j) = P(T+j)/P(T)\).
- **YTM** is a complicated weighted average of the spot rates for the bond's cash flows.
- A bond's **realized return** equals its YTM only if held to maturity, coupons are reinvested at the YTM and there's no default.
- If future spot rates turn out **exactly as today's forwards predict**, all bonds earn the one-period spot rate over the next period, whatever their maturity.

## Bootstrapping spot rates from the par curve
@id bootstrapping-spot
@r 23
@aka bootstrapping; deriving spot rates from par rates; zero coupon curve from coupon bonds; par curve to spot curve
@see spot-rate; par-rate

Solve for spot rates one maturity at a time. The 1-year par rate is the 1-year spot rate. For year 2, a par bond's coupon C₂ satisfies \(1 = \frac{C_2}{1+S_1} + \frac{1+C_2}{(1+S_2)^2}\); since S₁ is known, solve for S₂, and so on.

## Riding the yield curve
@id yield-curve-strategies
@r 23
@aka riding the yield curve; rolling down the yield curve; roll down return; active bond management forward rates; spot rates evolving as forwards
@see spot-forward-ytm; yield-curve-shapes

If you believe future spot rates will be **lower than** current forward rates, buying bonds with maturities longer than your horizon (and selling before maturity) earns more than the forward-implied return.

**Riding (rolling down) the yield curve**: with an upward-sloping curve that stays unchanged, a bond's yield falls as it ages toward the shorter, lower-yield part of the curve, so its price rises; you earn more than buying a bond matching your horizon. It works best with a steep curve that stays put.

## Swap rate curve and swap spread
@id swap-curve
@r 23
@aka swap rate curve; swap curve; swap spread; why use the swap curve; I spread; swap curve as benchmark
@see yield-spreads; swap-pricing

The **swap rate curve** plots fixed rates on interest rate swaps by maturity. It's widely used as a benchmark because swaps trade in many maturities, are liquid, and reflect bank-level credit risk; governments' curves are affected by supply, regulation and safe-haven demand, and many countries lack a liquid government curve.

**Swap spread** = swap rate − government bond yield of the same maturity. It reflects bank credit and liquidity risk and supply and demand. The **I-spread** of a bond is its yield minus the swap rate.

## Short-term spreads: TED and Libor-OIS
@id short-term-spreads
@r 23
@aka TED spread; Libor OIS spread; SOFR spread; overnight indexed swap; interbank stress indicator; funding stress; credit and liquidity risk indicators
@see swap-curve; credit-risk

Indicators of economy-wide credit and liquidity risk:

- **TED spread**: short-term bank borrowing rate (historically 3-month Libor) − T-bill rate. Widens in banking stress.
- **Libor-OIS spread**: Libor − overnight indexed swap rate (an expected policy rate with little credit risk). A cleaner measure of bank credit risk.

With Libor replaced by **SOFR** (a secured overnight rate), markets watch spreads between unsecured bank funding rates and SOFR, or SOFR swap spreads, for similar signals.

## Term structure theories
@id term-structure-theories
@r 23
@aka expectations theory; unbiased expectations; local expectations theory; liquidity preference theory; segmented markets theory; preferred habitat theory; term premium
@see yield-curve-shapes; forward-rate

| Theory | Says | Forward rates are |
|---|---|---|
| Unbiased (pure) expectations | Forwards = expected future spot rates | Unbiased predictors |
| Local expectations | Short-term returns on all bonds equal the risk-free rate (over short horizons) | Unbiased locally |
| Liquidity preference | Investors demand a **term (liquidity) premium** for longer maturities | Expected spot + premium (biased up) |
| Segmented markets | Each maturity is set by its own supply and demand | No relationship |
| Preferred habitat | Investors prefer a maturity but will move for enough extra yield | Expected + premium that can be positive or negative |

Under liquidity preference, an upward-sloping curve doesn't necessarily mean rates are expected to rise.

## Yield curve factors: level, steepness, curvature
@id yield-curve-factors
@r 23
@aka level slope curvature; parallel shift; steepening; flattening; butterfly; key rate duration; yield curve risk management; shaping risk
@see key-rate-duration; ml-unsupervised

Changes in the yield curve are well explained by three factors (identified with principal components analysis):

- **Level**: parallel shifts (explains most of the movement).
- **Steepness (slope)**: short and long rates move in opposite directions.
- **Curvature**: the middle moves relative to the ends (butterfly).

Measure exposures with **key rate durations** or factor durations; a portfolio's predicted change ≈ −Σ(exposure × factor move). Hedge by matching exposures, not just total duration.

## Yield volatility term structure
@id yield-volatility
@r 23
@aka term structure of volatility; short rate volatility; yield volatility by maturity; price volatility and yield volatility
@see yield-curve-factors

Short-term yields are usually **more volatile** than long-term yields: they react directly to monetary policy, while long-term yields reflect expectations about inflation and real rates averaged over long periods. A bond's price volatility ≈ duration × yield volatility, so long bonds still have more price risk despite lower yield volatility.

## Macro drivers of rates, spreads and curve shape
@id macro-rate-views
@r 23, 34
@aka monetary policy and yield curve; fiscal policy yield curve; inflation expectations; developing views on rates; yield curve and business cycle; bond market view
@see yield-curve-shapes; monetary-policy; business-cycle-assets

Views on benchmark yields come from: **monetary policy** (short rates; QE affects long rates), **inflation expectations**, **real growth**, **fiscal policy** (deficits raise supply and term premiums), and **flight to quality**. Typical patterns: tightening flattens the curve (short rates up); easing steepens it; a weakening economy lowers rates and widens credit spreads; supply of long bonds raises term premiums.

## Arbitrage-free valuation
@id arbitrage-free-valuation
@r 24
@aka arbitrage free valuation; law of one price bonds; value additivity; dominance; stripping; reconstitution; pricing with spot rates
@see cash-flow-additivity; binomial-rate-tree

A bond is priced **arbitrage-free** when its price equals the sum of its cash flows each discounted at the appropriate **spot** rate. Otherwise you could profit by **stripping** (selling the cash flows separately) or **reconstitution** (buying the pieces and assembling the bond). Two arbitrage principles: **value additivity** (the whole equals the sum of the parts) and **dominance** (a security with a higher payoff in every state must cost more).

## Binomial interest rate tree
@id binomial-rate-tree
@r 24
@aka binomial interest rate tree; lognormal random walk interest rates; one period forward rates tree; calibration; backward induction; interest rate volatility tree
@see arbitrage-free-valuation; embedded-option-valuation; pathwise-valuation

A lattice of possible future **one-period** rates. Under a lognormal model, adjacent rates at a node are related by \(r_{u} = r_{d} e^{2\sigma}\) (σ = rate volatility). Each move up or down has probability 0.5.

**Calibration**: choose the rates so the tree prices the benchmark (on-the-run) bonds exactly; then it's arbitrage-free.

**Backward induction**: start at maturity; at each node, value = average of the two next-period values (each including the coupon) discounted at that node's rate:

$$V = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}(V_u + C) + \tfrac{1}{2}(V_d + C)}{1 + r_{node}}$$

For an option-free bond, the tree gives the same value as discounting at spot rates; its power is valuing bonds whose cash flows depend on rates.

## Pathwise valuation and Monte Carlo
@id pathwise-valuation
@r 24
@aka pathwise valuation; interest rate paths; Monte Carlo interest rate simulation; path dependent securities; MBS valuation simulation; drift adjustment
@see binomial-rate-tree; monte-carlo

**Pathwise valuation**: list every possible rate path through the tree, value the bond along each path (discounting at that path's rates), and average. Equivalent to backward induction for path-independent bonds.

**Monte Carlo**: simulate many random rate paths (calibrated to the current curve with a drift adjustment); value cash flows along each and average. Necessary for **path-dependent** securities like mortgage-backed securities, where prepayments depend on the history of rates.

## Term structure models
@id term-structure-models
@r 24
@aka term structure models; equilibrium models; arbitrage free models; Cox Ingersoll Ross; CIR; Vasicek; Ho Lee; Kalotay Williams Fabozzi; short rate models; mean reversion interest rates
@see binomial-rate-tree

Models of how the short rate evolves:

- **Equilibrium models** (derived from economic assumptions; don't exactly fit today's curve):
  - **Cox-Ingersoll-Ross**: mean-reverting; volatility rises with the rate level, so rates can't go negative.
  - **Vasicek**: mean-reverting; constant volatility; rates can go negative.
- **Arbitrage-free models** (calibrated to match today's curve exactly):
  - **Ho-Lee**: normal rates, drift chosen to fit the curve.
  - **Kalotay-Williams-Fabozzi**: lognormal rates.

Practitioners prefer arbitrage-free models for valuing securities.

## Bonds with embedded options: value relationships
@id embedded-option-valuation
@r 25
@aka value of callable bond; value of putable bond; call option value; put option value; straight bond value; valuing callable bond with binomial tree; American Bermudan European call; extendible bond
@see embedded-options; binomial-rate-tree; oas

$$V_{callable} = V_{straight} - V_{call} \qquad V_{putable} = V_{straight} + V_{put}$$

To value with a binomial tree, apply the option at each node during backward induction: for a **callable** bond, the node value is the **lower** of the computed value and the call price (the issuer calls when the bond is worth more than the call price); for a **putable** bond, the **higher** of the computed value and the put price. An **extendible** bond is equivalent to a putable bond with the longer maturity.

## Interest rate volatility and the yield curve: effect on option value
@id volatility-embedded-options
@r 25
@aka volatility effect callable bond; volatility effect putable bond; yield curve shape and call option value; upward sloping curve callable
@see embedded-option-valuation

- **Volatility**: higher volatility increases the value of any option. So a **callable** bond's value **falls** and a **putable** bond's value **rises** as volatility rises.
- **Level of rates**: lower rates make the call more valuable (callable price is capped); higher rates make the put more valuable.
- **Shape of the curve**: an upward-sloping curve makes calls less valuable (rates are expected to rise) and puts more valuable; a flat or inverted curve does the opposite.

## Option-adjusted spread (OAS)
@id oas
@r 25
@aka OAS; option adjusted spread; Z spread vs OAS; option cost; constant spread added to tree; volatility and OAS; relative value bonds with options
@see yield-spreads; embedded-option-valuation

The **OAS** is the constant spread added to every rate in the (calibrated) binomial tree that makes the model value equal the bond's market price. It removes the effect of the embedded option, so it measures spread for credit and liquidity alone.

Option cost = Z-spread − OAS (positive for callable, negative for putable bonds).

Compare a bond's OAS with bonds of similar credit: a higher OAS suggests it's cheap. The OAS depends on the assumed volatility: for a callable bond, **higher assumed volatility → lower OAS** (the model values the call more, so less spread is needed to match the price).

## Effective duration of bonds with options; one-sided and key rate durations
@id effective-duration-options
@r 25
@aka effective duration callable bond; effective duration putable bond; one sided duration; one-sided up duration; one-sided down duration; key rate durations callable
@see effective-duration; key-rate-duration; effective-convexity-options

Compute effective duration by shifting the benchmark curve up and down, recalibrating the tree, and revaluing (holding OAS constant).

- **Callable** bond duration ≤ straight bond duration (much lower when rates are low and the call is likely).
- **Putable** bond duration ≤ straight bond duration (much lower when rates are high and the put is likely).
- Zero-coupon ≈ maturity; FRN ≈ time to reset.

**One-sided durations** capture asymmetry: callable bonds are more sensitive to **rising** rates (price can fall, but is capped when rates fall), putable bonds more sensitive to **falling** rates. **Key rate durations** show which maturities matter: a callable bond trading near its call price is most sensitive to the rate at the call date.

## Effective convexity of bonds with options
@id effective-convexity-options
@r 25
@aka effective convexity callable; negative convexity callable bond; positive convexity putable bond
@see effective-duration-options; negative-convexity

- **Straight bonds**: positive convexity.
- **Callable bonds**: **negative convexity** when rates are low (the call caps price gains); positive when rates are high.
- **Putable bonds**: always positive convexity, more than straight bonds when rates are high (the put floors the price).

## Capped and floored floating-rate bonds
@id capped-floored-floaters
@r 25
@aka capped floater; floored floater; interest rate cap; interest rate floor; collared floater; valuing capped FRN with tree
@see frn; embedded-option-valuation; interest-rate-options

A **cap** limits the floating coupon to a maximum rate: it benefits the **issuer**. Capped floater = straight floater − cap. A **floor** sets a minimum coupon, benefiting the **investor**: floored floater = straight floater + floor. Value in a binomial tree by setting each node's coupon to min(rate, cap) or max(rate, floor), paid in arrears (the coupon at a node is set by the previous period's rate).

## Convertible bonds
@id convertible-bonds
@r 25
@aka convertible bond; conversion ratio; conversion price; conversion value; market conversion premium; premium payback period; minimum value of convertible; busted convertible; mandatory convertible; contingent conversion
@see embedded-options; convertible-risk-return

A bond the holder can exchange for a fixed number of shares (**conversion ratio**).

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Conversion price | Issue price / conversion ratio |
| Conversion value | Share price × conversion ratio |
| Minimum value | max(straight bond value, conversion value) |
| Market conversion price | Convertible price / conversion ratio |
| Market conversion premium per share | Market conversion price − share price |
| Premium payback period | Premium per share / (coupon income − dividend income per share) |

Value ≈ straight bond + call option on the stock − any call option held by the issuer + any put held by the investor. Valued in an arbitrage-free framework (trees for both rates and the share price).

## Convertible bond risk-return profile
@id convertible-risk-return
@r 25
@aka convertible bond behavior; busted convertible; bond like convertible; equity like convertible; hybrid convertible; convertible arbitrage
@see convertible-bonds; hedge-fund-strategies-relative-value

- **Share price well below conversion price** ("busted"): behaves like a straight bond; sensitive to rates and credit.
- **Share price well above**: behaves like the stock (minus the premium).
- **In between**: hybrid; participates in upside, cushioned on the downside.

Compared with the stock: less upside (premium paid), more downside protection. Compared with the straight bond: lower coupon, but equity upside. Convertible arbitrage funds buy convertibles and short the stock to isolate cheap embedded options.

## Credit risk components: EE, LGD, PD, CVA
@id credit-valuation-adjustment
@r 26
@aka expected exposure; loss given default; probability of default; hazard rate; probability of survival; credit valuation adjustment; CVA; expected loss; recovery rate
@see credit-risk; credit-spread-models

- **Expected exposure (EE)**: what you'd be owed at the time of default.
- **Recovery rate** and **loss given default (LGD)** = EE × (1 − recovery rate).
- **Hazard rate**: probability of default in a period given survival so far; **probability of survival** = product of (1 − hazard) to date; **probability of default** in year t = hazard × survival to t − 1.
- **Expected loss** in a period = PD × LGD.

**CVA** = sum of the PVs of the expected losses over the bond's life (discounted at risk-free rates). Value of a risky bond = value if risk-free − CVA.

## Credit scores, ratings and rating transitions
@id credit-scores-ratings
@r 26
@aka credit scores; consumer credit scores; credit ratings; rating transition matrix; migration risk; expected return with rating changes; spread duration
@see credit-ratings; credit-valuation-adjustment

**Credit scores** rank consumers and small businesses by default risk; **ratings** do the same for corporations and governments. Both are ordinal, stable and slow-moving (they don't explain why).

A **rating transition matrix** gives the probabilities of moving between ratings over a year. Expected change in price from rating moves ≈ −Σ(probability × spread change × modified duration). Because downgrades widen spreads more than upgrades narrow them, expected migration losses reduce expected returns below the YTM.

## Structural and reduced-form credit models
@id credit-spread-models
@r 26
@aka structural model; Merton model; reduced form model; equity as call option on assets; default as option; distance to default; Jarrow Turnbull; implied default probability
@see credit-valuation-adjustment; option-value-factors

- **Structural models** (Merton): equity is a **call option** on the firm's assets with strike = face value of debt; debt = risk-free debt − a **put** on the assets. Default happens when assets fall below debt. Strengths: intuitive, explain why default happens. Weaknesses: firm asset value and volatility aren't observable; balance sheets are simplified.
- **Reduced-form models**: default is a random event with a hazard rate that may depend on macro and firm variables; no need to model asset values. Strengths: fit market data, use observable variables. Weaknesses: don't explain *why* default occurs; hazard rates are estimated from data that may not persist.

## Valuing risky bonds and interpreting credit spread changes
@id credit-spread-valuation
@r 26
@aka value of risky bond with tree; credit spread calculation; credit spread from CVA; change in default probability; change in recovery rate
@see credit-valuation-adjustment; binomial-rate-tree

Value a risky bond with an arbitrage-free tree that includes default: at each node, expected value = survival probability × (next values + coupon) + default probability × recovery. Its implied YTM minus the risk-free YTM is the **credit spread**. Spreads widen when the probability of default or loss given default rises, and when liquidity or risk aversion deteriorate.

## Term structure of credit spreads
@id credit-spread-term-structure
@r 26
@aka credit spread curve; term structure of credit spreads; upward sloping credit curve; inverted credit spread curve; distressed issuer credit curve
@see credit-spread-models; yield-spread-drivers

Drivers: credit quality, the economic cycle, market supply and demand, liquidity and company-specific factors.

- **Investment-grade issuers**: usually **upward-sloping** credit curves (default risk rises over longer horizons; also more uncertainty).
- **High-yield and distressed issuers**: can be **flat or inverted**: near-term default risk is high, and if the company survives the next few years, its credit should improve. Distressed bonds trade on price (recovery value) rather than spread.

Best measured from zero-coupon spreads (e.g. Z-spreads or CDS spreads at different maturities).

## Credit analysis of securitized debt
@id securitized-credit
@r 26
@aka credit analysis ABS; securitized debt credit; static pool; dynamic pool; servicer quality; structure and tranching credit; granularity; homogeneity
@see securitization; credit-enhancement

Unlike corporate credit, securitized debt depends on the **collateral pool** and **structure**, not an operating business. Analyse: **homogeneity and granularity** of the pool (many small similar loans are easier to model), **static vs revolving** pools, the **servicer's** quality, **credit enhancement** and the waterfall, and the legal structure (true sale, bankruptcy remoteness).

## Credit default swaps: structure and parameters
@id cds-structure
@r 27
@aka credit default swap; single name CDS; index CDS; CDX; iTraxx; tranche CDS; reference obligation; protection buyer; protection seller; CDS spread; standard coupon; upfront payment; notional
@see credit-derivatives; cds-pricing

A CDS transfers credit risk from the **protection buyer** to the **protection seller**. The buyer pays a periodic premium; the seller compensates for losses after a **credit event**.

- **Single-name CDS**: one reference entity; the **cheapest-to-deliver** obligation of the same seniority determines the payout.
- **Index CDS** (e.g. CDX, iTraxx): an equally weighted portfolio of names; each default reduces notional.
- **Tranche CDS**: covers a slice of losses on an index.

Contracts pay standard coupons (e.g. 1% for investment grade, 5% for high yield); the difference from the fair spread is settled with an **upfront payment**.

## CDS credit events and settlement
@id cds-credit-events
@r 27
@aka credit events; bankruptcy; failure to pay; restructuring credit event; determinations committee; physical settlement; cash settlement; CDS auction; succession event
@see cds-structure

**Credit events**: bankruptcy, failure to pay, and (in some contracts) **restructuring** (changes in terms that harm holders). A **determinations committee** decides whether an event occurred. **Succession events** (mergers, spin-offs) can change the reference entity.

Settlement: **physical** (buyer delivers the bonds for par) or **cash** (payment = notional × (1 − recovery rate), with recovery set by an **auction**).

## CDS pricing
@id cds-pricing
@r 27
@aka CDS pricing; CDS spread; hazard rate CDS; upfront premium; points upfront; CDS duration; change in CDS value; protection leg; premium leg
@see cds-structure; credit-valuation-adjustment

A CDS has a **protection leg** (expected payouts on default) and a **premium leg** (expected premiums). The fair spread equalizes their PVs. Approximately:

$$\text{CDS spread} \approx \text{hazard rate} \times LGD$$

Upfront payment ≈ (CDS spread − fixed coupon) × CDS duration (as % of notional). Price per 100 = 100 − upfront %. If the spread widens, the protection **buyer gains**: change in value ≈ change in spread × duration × notional.

## Uses of CDS
@id cds-uses
@r 27
@aka CDS strategies; hedging credit risk; long protection; short protection; curve trade CDS; basis trade; CDS bond basis; long short credit trade; index arbitrage
@see cds-pricing

- **Manage exposure**: buy protection to hedge a bond or loan; sell protection to gain credit exposure without buying bonds.
- **Express views**: buy protection on a name you expect to deteriorate; **long/short** trades between two names; **curve trades** (e.g. buy long-dated and sell short-dated protection if you expect the credit curve to steepen).
- **Exploit pricing differences**: the **basis trade** exploits a gap between a bond's spread and the CDS spread (buy the bond and buy protection if the bond's spread is wider); index vs single-name arbitrage; equity vs credit (e.g. LBO candidates: buy protection, buy stock).
