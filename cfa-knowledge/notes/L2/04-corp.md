## Effects of dividends, splits and stock dividends
@id dividend-forms-effects
@r 13
@aka regular dividend; extra dividend; special dividend; liquidating dividend; stock dividend effect; stock split effect; reverse split; dividend and share price
@see dividends-buybacks; dividend-policy-theories

- **Regular cash dividends**: reduce cash and equity; signal stability. Price drops by about the dividend on the ex-date.
- **Extra (special) dividends**: one-off; avoid committing to a higher regular level.
- **Liquidating dividends**: return of capital when a business is wound down or part sold.
- **Stock dividends and stock splits**: more shares, same total value; share price falls proportionally. No change in shareholder wealth (except possibly signalling or liquidity effects).
- **Reverse splits**: fewer shares at a higher price, often to meet exchange listing minimums.

## Dividend policy theories
@id dividend-policy-theories
@r 13
@aka dividend irrelevance; MM dividend irrelevance; bird in the hand; tax aversion; clientele effect; dividend policy matters; homemade dividends
@see modigliani-miller; dividend-signaling; dividend-forms-effects

- **Dividend irrelevance (MM)**: in perfect markets, dividend policy doesn't affect value; investors can create "homemade dividends" by selling shares.
- **Bird-in-the-hand**: investors value certain dividends more than uncertain future gains, so higher payout → lower cost of equity and higher value.
- **Tax aversion**: where dividends are taxed more heavily than capital gains, investors prefer lower payout (and buybacks).
- **Clientele effect**: different investor groups (tax status, income needs) prefer different policies; firms attract a clientele, so changing policy can upset holders.

## Signals from dividend changes
@id dividend-signaling
@r 13
@aka dividend signaling; dividend initiation; dividend increase signal; dividend cut; dividend omission; information asymmetry dividends
@see dividend-policy-theories; agency-costs-payout

Managers know more than investors, so dividend changes carry information. **Initiations and increases** usually signal confidence in sustainable future earnings (prices tend to rise); **cuts and omissions** usually signal trouble (prices tend to fall), though a cut to fund attractive investments can be read positively. Because cutting is painful, managers raise dividends only when confident, which gives the signal its credibility.

## Agency costs and payout policy
@id agency-costs-payout
@r 13
@aka agency costs dividends; free cash flow hypothesis; overinvestment; empire building; dividends discipline managers; bondholder shareholder conflict dividends
@see principal-agent; dividend-signaling

Paying out cash reduces the free cash flow managers could waste on value-destroying projects (the **free cash flow hypothesis**), which is why investors often welcome higher payouts from firms with few good projects. On the other side, paying large dividends financed by debt can transfer wealth from **bondholders** to shareholders, so bond covenants often restrict payouts.

## Factors affecting dividend policy in practice
@id dividend-policy-factors
@r 13
@aka factors affecting dividend policy; investment opportunities; earnings volatility; financial flexibility; tax considerations; flotation costs; contractual restrictions; legal restrictions on dividends
@see dividend-policy-theories; payout-policies

- **Investment opportunities**: more good projects → lower payout.
- **Expected earnings volatility**: volatile earnings → lower, more cautious dividends.
- **Financial flexibility**: firms keep cash and prefer buybacks to avoid committing.
- **Tax considerations**: dividend vs capital gains taxes for shareholders.
- **Flotation costs**: issuing new equity is costly, so firms avoid paying dividends they then need to replace with new shares.
- **Contractual and legal restrictions**: covenants, capital impairment rules, solvency tests.

## Effective tax rate on dividends
@id dividend-tax-systems
@r 13
@aka double taxation; dividend imputation; split rate tax system; effective tax rate on dividends; corporate tax and dividend tax combined
@see dividend-policy-factors

The combined tax on a unit of pre-tax corporate earnings paid as a dividend:

- **Double taxation**: \(1 - (1 - t_c)(1 - t_{div})\). Earnings are taxed at the corporate rate, then dividends taxed again.
- **Dividend imputation**: shareholders get a credit for corporate tax paid, so earnings are effectively taxed once at the shareholder's rate.
- **Split-rate**: distributed earnings are taxed at a lower corporate rate than retained earnings, then taxed again as dividends.

## Stable dividend vs. constant payout ratio
@id payout-policies
@r 13
@aka stable dividend policy; constant payout ratio; target payout adjustment model; residual dividend policy; dividend smoothing; Lintner model
@see dividend-policy-factors

- **Stable dividend policy**: dividends smoothed relative to earnings, raised gradually toward a target payout. The target payout adjustment model: \(\Delta D = (\text{expected EPS} \times \text{target payout} - D_{prev}) \times \text{adjustment factor}\).
- **Constant payout ratio**: a fixed % of earnings; dividends as volatile as earnings. Rarely used.
- **Residual dividend policy**: pay what's left after funding all positive-NPV projects with the target capital structure; very volatile.

Most firms smooth dividends; trends include a falling share of firms paying dividends and a rising share of buybacks in total payouts (especially in the US).

## Share repurchase methods
@id buyback-methods
@r 13
@aka share repurchase methods; open market repurchase; fixed price tender offer; Dutch auction; direct negotiation; accelerated share repurchase; greenmail
@see dividends-buybacks; buyback-eps-bvps

- **Open market**: buy on the exchange over time; flexible and cheapest; most common.
- **Fixed-price tender offer**: offer to buy a fixed number of shares at a premium within a period; fast.
- **Dutch auction tender**: shareholders submit prices within a range; the company buys at the lowest price that fills the target amount.
- **Direct negotiation** with a large holder (e.g. to remove an activist; "greenmail" at a premium harms other holders).

## Share repurchases: EPS and book value effects
@id buyback-eps-bvps
@r 13
@aka buyback EPS accretion; repurchase financed with debt; earnings yield vs after tax cost of debt; book value per share effect of buyback; dividend vs buyback choice
@see buyback-methods; dividends-buybacks

**EPS**: a debt-financed buyback **raises EPS** if the **earnings yield** (E/P) exceeds the **after-tax cost of debt**; otherwise it dilutes. A buyback from surplus cash raises EPS if the earnings yield exceeds the after-tax return earned on that cash.

**Book value per share**: falls if the buyback price is above BVPS (market-to-book > 1), rises if below.

A buyback at market price leaves wealth unchanged for remaining shareholders (same as an equal cash dividend) in a world without taxes and signalling. Reasons to prefer buybacks: flexibility, tax efficiency, signalling undervaluation, offsetting option dilution, adjusting leverage. Reasons to prefer dividends: commitment, clientele preferences.

## Dividend coverage and sustainability
@id dividend-coverage
@r 13
@aka dividend coverage ratio; dividend sustainability; FCFE coverage ratio; payout ratio; can company afford dividend; dividend cut risk
@see free-cash-flow; dividend-signaling

- **Dividend coverage** = net income / dividends (inverse of the payout ratio).
- **FCFE coverage** = FCFE / (dividends + buybacks). Below 1 means the company is paying out more than it generates, funded by debt or cash.

Companies that may not sustain dividends: payout ratios near or above 100%, FCFE coverage below 1, rising leverage to fund dividends, declining earnings, very high dividend yields relative to peers (the market may be expecting a cut).

## Ownership structures and corporate governance
@id ownership-governance
@r 14
@aka dispersed ownership; concentrated ownership; controlling shareholder; principal principal conflict; family owned firms; dual class shares governance; cross shareholdings; pyramid structures; state ownership
@see corporate-governance; principal-agent

- **Dispersed ownership** (many small holders): main conflict is shareholders vs managers (principal-agent).
- **Concentrated ownership** (a family, the state, a parent company): main conflict is **controlling vs minority shareholders** (principal-principal); expropriation through related-party deals, unequal voting rights.

Mechanisms that reinforce control: **dual-class shares**, **pyramid structures**, **cross-shareholdings**. Assess governance by board independence and expertise, committee structure, executive pay alignment, shareholder rights, and the treatment of minority holders.

## Evaluating ESG risks and opportunities
@id esg-analysis
@r 14
@aka ESG analysis; materiality; ESG risk exposure; ESG integration in valuation; carbon pricing; stranded assets; ESG adjustments to cash flows; ESG and cost of capital
@see esg-factors; esg-investing

Identify **material** ESG issues for the industry (e.g. emissions for utilities, data privacy for tech, labour conditions in apparel supply chains) using frameworks and peer comparisons. Then reflect them in valuation:

- **Cash flows**: higher compliance costs, carbon taxes, capex for transition, fines; revenue opportunities from green products.
- **Balance sheet**: impairments of stranded assets.
- **Discount rate or multiples**: higher risk premium for poorly managed exposures.

## Top-down and bottom-up drivers of the cost of capital
@id cost-of-capital-drivers
@r 15
@aka cost of capital factors; top down factors; bottom up factors; country risk; capital availability; market conditions; legal environment; revenue variability; operating leverage cost of capital
@see wacc; cost-of-capital-l2

- **Top-down (external)**: availability of capital (deep, developed markets → lower cost), market conditions (risk-free rate, risk premiums, inflation), legal and regulatory environment (creditor and investor protection), country risk, tax rates.
- **Bottom-up (company)**: revenue and earnings volatility, business model and operating leverage, asset nature (tangible collateral), financial leverage, liquidity of the securities, and stage in the life cycle.

## Estimating the cost of debt
@id cost-of-debt-l2
@r 15
@aka cost of debt methods; yield to maturity approach; debt rating approach; synthetic rating; interest coverage rating; cost of debt for private company; bank loans; leases cost of debt
@see cost-of-debt; wacc

Methods:

- **YTM of traded bonds** (best when liquid bonds exist).
- **Debt rating approach**: yields of bonds with the same rating and maturity.
- **Synthetic rating**: estimate a rating from financial ratios (e.g. interest coverage), then use that rating's yield. Used for private or unrated firms.
- Non-traded debt (bank loans) and leases: use comparable rates.

Include floating-rate and short-term debt; adjust to after-tax. For distressed debt, the promised YTM overstates the expected cost (include expected default losses).

## Estimating the equity risk premium
@id equity-risk-premium
@r 15
@aka equity risk premium; ERP; historical equity risk premium; forward looking ERP; Gordon growth ERP; supply side model; Ibbotson Chen; survey approach; arithmetic vs geometric mean ERP
@see cost-of-equity; capm; cost-of-capital-l2

- **Historical**: average excess return of equities over government bonds or bills. Choices: period length, geometric vs arithmetic mean, bills vs bonds. Suffers from survivorship bias and assumes the past repeats.
- **Forward-looking (ex ante)**:
  - **Gordon growth** (implied): ERP = dividend yield + expected growth − long-term government yield.
  - **Supply-side (macro) model**: ERP from expected inflation, real EPS growth, growth in P/E and income return, minus the risk-free rate.
  - **Surveys** of experts or investors.

## Required return on equity: models
@id cost-of-capital-l2
@r 15, 22
@aka required return on equity methods; CAPM; multifactor model; Fama French; build up approach; bond yield plus risk premium; country risk premium; size premium; private company cost of equity
@see cost-of-equity; equity-risk-premium; private-company-discount-rate

- **CAPM** with an estimated beta (regression, or peer betas unlevered and relevered).
- **Multifactor models** (e.g. Fama-French, adding size and value).
- **Build-up approach** (often for private firms): risk-free rate + ERP + **size premium** + company-specific premium (+ industry premium).
- **Bond yield plus risk premium**: company's bond yield + a few percent.
- **Country risk premium** for emerging markets, e.g. sovereign spread × (equity volatility / bond volatility).

**Beta for a private or non-traded company (pure-play method)**: unlever comparable firms' betas, \(\beta_A = \beta_E/[1 + (1-t)D/E]\), then relever at the target's capital structure.

## Types of corporate restructuring
@id corporate-restructuring
@r 16
@aka corporate restructuring; mergers and acquisitions; M&A; divestitures; spin off; equity carve out; split off; joint venture; cost restructuring; balance sheet restructuring; leveraged recapitalization
@see restructuring-valuation; business-combinations

Three groups:

- **Investment actions**: equity investments (minority stakes), joint ventures, acquisitions (to grow, gain synergies, buy capabilities).
- **Divestment actions**: sales of divisions, **spin-offs** (distribute a subsidiary's shares to existing shareholders), **carve-outs** (sell part of a subsidiary in an IPO).
- **Restructuring actions**: **cost restructuring** (cut costs, outsource, close plants), **balance sheet restructuring** (sale-leasebacks, changes in capital structure, buybacks, leveraged recaps), **reorganization** (bankruptcy).

Motivations: change in strategy, under-valued parts ("sum of the parts" > market value), synergies, activist pressure, regulatory or tax reasons.

## Valuing companies in restructurings
@id restructuring-valuation
@r 16
@aka comparable company analysis; comparable transaction analysis; premiums paid analysis; DCF for M&A; synergies valuation; takeover premium; sum of the parts; accretion dilution
@see corporate-restructuring; ev-multiples; sum-of-the-parts

Methods:

- **Comparable company analysis**: trading multiples of peers; add a **takeover premium** for a control transaction.
- **Comparable transaction analysis**: multiples paid in recent acquisitions (already include a premium); limited by few deals and different conditions.
- **Premiums paid analysis**: typical premiums over pre-announcement prices in similar deals.
- **DCF**: value the target with and without **synergies**; the most flexible but most assumption-dependent.

Effects to analyse: **EPS accretion/dilution** (depends on the acquirer's P/E vs the target's and on financing), the **net debt to EBITDA** of the combined firm, and the change in **WACC** (business risk and leverage). Investment actions are evaluated on whether NPV of the deal (including synergies) exceeds the premium paid.

## Evaluating divestitures, spin-offs and cost restructurings
@id divestment-evaluation
@r 16
@aka divestiture evaluation; spin off analysis; carve out; sale of division; cost restructuring evaluation; sale leaseback; balance sheet restructuring evaluation
@see corporate-restructuring; restructuring-valuation

- **Sales and spin-offs**: is the unit worth more to someone else or on its own? Consider the price vs the unit's standalone value, the parent's use of proceeds, tax costs, stranded costs left with the parent, and lost synergies. Spin-offs are usually tax-free and avoid a sale discount.
- **Cost restructuring**: compare the one-off costs (severance, closure) with the PV of future savings; check whether savings are sustainable or just deferred investment.
- **Balance sheet restructuring**: sale-leasebacks release cash but add lease liabilities; leveraged recaps raise EPS and ROE but increase financial risk.
