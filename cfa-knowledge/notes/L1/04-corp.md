## Organizational forms of business
@id organizational-forms
@r 20
@aka sole proprietorship; partnership; general partnership; limited partnership; corporation; limited liability; legal entity; pass through taxation; double taxation
@see public-vs-private-companies

| Form | Owner liability | Taxation | Access to capital |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Unlimited | Owner's income | Owner's own funds and loans |
| General partnership | Unlimited, shared | Pass-through to partners | Partners |
| Limited partnership | GP unlimited, LPs limited | Pass-through | GP + LPs (common in private equity funds) |
| Corporation | Limited | Corporate tax, then tax on dividends ("double taxation" in many countries) | Equity and debt markets |

A **corporation** is a separate legal entity: it can own assets, sign contracts and borrow; owners and managers can differ; shares transfer easily; it can live forever.

## Public vs. private companies
@id public-vs-private-companies
@r 20, 39
@aka listed company; private company; going public; IPO; direct listing; SPAC; take private; leveraged buyout; delisting
@see ipo; private-equity

**Public** companies' shares trade on exchanges: easy to buy and sell, strict disclosure rules, broad shareholder base. **Private** companies' shares are not listed: illiquid, fewer disclosure requirements, concentrated owners, often longer-term horizons.

Routes from private to public: an **IPO**, a **direct listing** (existing shares listed without raising new money), or a merger with a **SPAC** (a listed shell company). Routes from public to private: a buyout, often a **leveraged buyout** by private equity or a management buyout.

## Lenders vs. shareholders
@id debt-vs-equity-claims
@r 21
@aka creditors vs shareholders; debt holders vs equity holders; residual claim; upside potential; conflicts between bondholders and shareholders; asymmetric payoff
@see principal-agent; capital-structure

**Lenders** have a fixed, contractual claim: interest and principal, with priority in bankruptcy. Their upside is capped, so they care about the downside: stable cash flows, collateral and low risk. **Shareholders** have the residual claim: unlimited upside, limited downside (they can lose only what they invested). Shareholders therefore prefer more risk and more leverage than lenders do, which is a core source of conflict.

## Stakeholders
@id stakeholders
@r 21, 22
@aka stakeholder groups; shareholders; creditors; managers; employees; board of directors; customers; suppliers; government; stakeholder theory; shareholder theory
@see principal-agent; corporate-governance; esg-factors

Anyone with an interest in a company: shareholders, creditors, the board, managers, employees, customers, suppliers and governments/regulators. **Shareholder theory** says the company's main duty is to maximize shareholder value; **stakeholder theory** says it should balance the interests of all stakeholders.

## ESG factors of corporate issuers
@id esg-factors
@r 21, 90
@aka ESG; environmental social governance; sustainability; climate risk; greenwashing; ESG integration; stranded assets; human capital; responsible investing
@see esg-investing; corporate-governance

Non-financial factors that can affect a company's risk and value:

- **Environmental**: climate change, carbon emissions, pollution, water use, resource scarcity, stranded assets.
- **Social**: labour practices, human capital, product safety, data privacy, supply-chain conditions, community relations.
- **Governance**: board independence, executive pay, shareholder rights, accounting quality, ethics.

ESG issues are **material** when they can affect cash flows, the cost of capital or reputation. Analysts face problems of inconsistent data, different ratings across providers and **greenwashing** (overstated claims).

## Principal-agent problem
@id principal-agent
@r 22
@aka agency problem; agency costs; manager vs shareholder conflict; empire building; perquisites; information asymmetry; controlling shareholder conflict
@see corporate-governance; stakeholders

When one party (the **agent**, e.g. managers) acts for another (the **principal**, e.g. shareholders) but has its own interests and more information. Common conflicts:

- **Shareholders vs managers**: excessive pay and perks, empire building, avoiding risk to protect jobs (or taking too much to boost options), entrenchment.
- **Controlling vs minority shareholders**: related-party deals, unequal voting rights.
- **Shareholders vs creditors**: shareholders favour risky projects, higher dividends and more borrowing.
- **Managers/board vs other stakeholders**: customers, employees, suppliers, the public.

The costs of monitoring and bonding agents, and of remaining misbehaviour, are **agency costs**.

## Corporate governance mechanisms
@id corporate-governance
@r 22
@aka board of directors; independent directors; audit committee; compensation committee; say on pay; shareholder activism; proxy fight; takeover; covenants; good governance
@see principal-agent; voting-rights

The rules and processes by which a company is directed and controlled to manage stakeholder conflicts.

- **Shareholders**: annual meetings and votes, proxy voting, activism, the threat of takeovers.
- **Board of directors**: independent directors, separation of chair and CEO, committees (audit, compensation/remuneration, nominations, risk).
- **Creditors**: covenants in loan agreements and bond indentures.
- **Employees**: labour laws, contracts, unions.
- **Customers and suppliers**: contracts.
- **Government**: laws, regulations, governance codes.

Good governance lowers the cost of capital and improves operating results; poor governance raises legal, regulatory and reputational risk and can lead to bad decisions and fraud.

## Cash conversion cycle
@id cash-conversion-cycle
@r 23, 37
@aka CCC; cash cycle; operating cycle; days sales outstanding; DSO; days inventory; DIO; days payables; DPO; net operating cycle
@see working-capital; activity-ratios

How long cash is tied up in operations, from paying suppliers to collecting from customers.

$$CCC = DIO + DSO - DPO$$

| Component | Formula |
|---|---|
| Days inventory on hand (DIO) | 365 / inventory turnover = inventory / (COGS/365) |
| Days sales outstanding (DSO) | receivables / (revenue/365) |
| Days payables outstanding (DPO) | payables / (purchases/365) |

A shorter cycle means less financing is needed for working capital. Retailers with fast inventory and customer cash payment can have a **negative** CCC: suppliers fund their operations.

## Liquidity
@id liquidity
@r 23
@aka liquidity sources; primary sources of liquidity; secondary sources; drag on liquidity; pull on liquidity; cash management; ability to pay short term obligations
@see liquidity-ratios; working-capital; short-term-funding

A company's ability to meet short-term obligations. **Primary sources**: cash on hand, cash from operations, short-term funding (credit lines, trade credit). **Secondary sources**: renegotiating debt, selling assets, bankruptcy protection; these signal stress and usually cost more.

**Drags** slow cash coming in (slow collections, obsolete inventory); **pulls** speed cash going out (suppliers demanding faster payment, reduced credit lines).

## Working capital management
@id working-capital
@r 23, 48
@aka net working capital; conservative vs aggressive working capital policy; trade credit; early payment discount; 2/10 net 30; factoring; inventory management
@see cash-conversion-cycle; liquidity

Managing current assets and liabilities to balance liquidity against profitability. A **conservative** policy holds more current assets and funds them with long-term capital (safer, more costly); an **aggressive** policy holds less and uses more short-term funding (cheaper, riskier).

> **Example** Terms of "2/10 net 30": a 2% discount for paying in 10 days, otherwise pay in 30. Skipping the discount costs (1 + 2/98)^(365/20) − 1 ≈ 44.6% a year, so take it unless your funding costs more.

## Capital investments (types of projects)
@id capital-investments
@r 24
@aka capital budgeting; capex; going concern projects; regulatory projects; expansion projects; maintenance; replacement; new market
@see npv; irr; capital-allocation-principles

Capital investments are categorized as:

- **Going concern projects**: maintenance and replacement to keep the business running, or to cut costs.
- **Regulatory / compliance projects**: required by law or regulators.
- **Expansion projects**: growing existing businesses.
- **Other**: new products or markets, often riskier and harder to evaluate.

## Net present value (NPV)
@id npv
@r 24
@aka net present value; NPV rule; is the project worth it; value created by a project; discounted cash flow; DCF
@see irr; wacc; capital-allocation-principles; tvm

The present value of a project's expected cash flows minus its initial cost, discounted at the project's required return (often the [[wacc]]).

$$NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{N}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

**Accept if NPV > 0**: the project earns more than the cost of capital and should raise shareholder value by its NPV. For mutually exclusive projects choose the highest NPV. NPV is the preferred rule because it measures value directly and assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital.

> **Example** Cost 1,000; cash flows of 400 a year for 3 years; r = 10%. NPV = −1,000 + 400/1.1 + 400/1.21 + 400/1.331 = −5.3. Reject (just).

## Internal rate of return (IRR)
@id irr
@r 24, 3
@aka internal rate of return; IRR rule; hurdle rate; breakeven discount rate; NPV vs IRR conflict; multiple IRRs
@see npv; mwr-twr

The discount rate at which NPV = 0. Accept if IRR > the required return (hurdle rate).

$$0 = \sum_{t=0}^{N}\frac{CF_t}{(1+IRR)^t}$$

> **Watch out** NPV and IRR can rank mutually exclusive projects differently when projects differ in size or in the timing of cash flows. IRR assumes reinvestment at the IRR itself, which is often unrealistic. Unconventional cash flows (signs changing more than once) can give several IRRs or none. When they conflict, follow NPV.

## Return on invested capital (ROIC)
@id roic
@r 24, 48
@aka ROIC; return on capital; ROCE; value creation; ROIC vs WACC; economic profit
@see wacc; npv; profitability-ratios

After-tax operating profit divided by the capital invested (debt + equity, or net operating assets).

$$ROIC = \frac{EBIT(1-t)}{\text{average invested capital}}$$

A company creates value when **ROIC > cost of capital**. Unlike NPV, it is an accounting measure for a period, so it is sensitive to accounting choices and to the age of assets.

## Capital allocation principles and pitfalls
@id capital-allocation-principles
@r 24
@aka capital budgeting principles; incremental cash flows; sunk costs; opportunity costs; externalities; cannibalization; pitfalls; financing costs not in cash flows
@see npv; real-options

Principles for estimating project cash flows:

- Use **incremental, after-tax cash flows**, not accounting income.
- Ignore **sunk costs** (already spent, can't be recovered).
- Include **opportunity costs** (e.g. using a building you could otherwise rent out).
- Include **externalities**, such as **cannibalization** of existing product sales.
- Timing matters.
- **Exclude financing costs** from cash flows; they are captured in the discount rate.

Common pitfalls: inertia (reusing last year's budget), basing decisions on EPS or ROE effects rather than NPV, pet projects, internal forecasting biases, not considering alternatives, and failing to account for inflation consistently.

## Real options
@id real-options
@r 24
@aka timing option; abandonment option; expansion option; flexibility option; fundamental option; option to wait; strategic value
@see npv; capital-allocation-principles

Choices management can make after a project starts, which add value beyond a static NPV:

- **Timing**: delay the investment until more is known.
- **Abandonment**: stop if things go badly.
- **Expansion**: invest more if things go well.
- **Flexibility**: price-setting or production options (switch inputs or outputs).
- **Fundamental**: the project itself is option-like (e.g. a mine worth developing only if commodity prices are high).

Project NPV = NPV without options + value of options − cost of options.

## Weighted average cost of capital (WACC)
@id wacc
@r 25
@aka WACC; cost of capital; hurdle rate; discount rate for the firm; after tax cost of debt; cost of equity; capital weights; target capital structure
@see cost-of-equity; cost-of-debt; capital-structure; npv

The average return a company must earn for all its providers of capital, weighted by their share of the capital structure (use target or market-value weights).

$$WACC = w_d\,r_d(1-t) + w_p\,r_p + w_e\,r_e$$

Debt is tax-deductible, so its after-tax cost is used. WACC is the discount rate for projects with the same risk as the company's average project and for [[fcff]] valuations.

> **Example** 40% debt at 6% pre-tax, 25% tax, 60% equity at 11%: WACC = 0.4 × 6% × 0.75 + 0.6 × 11% = 1.8% + 6.6% = 8.4%.

## Cost of equity
@id cost-of-equity
@r 25, 50, 44
@aka required return on equity; CAPM cost of equity; bond yield plus risk premium; dividend discount model cost of equity; equity risk premium
@see capm; wacc; gordon-growth-model

The return shareholders require. It can't be observed, so it is estimated:

- **CAPM**: \(r_e = R_f + \beta(E(R_m) - R_f)\).
- **Dividend discount model**: \(r_e = D_1/P_0 + g\).
- **Bond yield plus risk premium**: the company's own bond yield plus 3–5%.

Cost of equity > cost of debt for the same firm, because equity holders bear more risk.

## Cost of debt
@id cost-of-debt
@r 25
@aka cost of debt capital; after tax cost of debt; yield to maturity of company bonds; debt rating approach; pre tax cost of debt
@see wacc; ytm

The rate a company would pay to borrow today (not the historic coupon). Use the [[ytm]] of its existing bonds, or for unrated or unlisted issuers the yield on comparable bonds with the same rating and maturity. After-tax cost = \(r_d(1-t)\), since interest is tax deductible.

## Capital structure factors
@id capital-structure
@r 25
@aka debt to equity; leverage decision; how much debt; optimal capital structure; target capital structure; company life cycle and leverage; pecking order theory
@see modigliani-miller; optimal-capital-structure; wacc

What drives how much debt a company uses:

- **Internal**: business model and stability of cash flows, asset tangibility (collateral), existing leverage, corporate tax rate, ownership and governance, the stage in its life cycle (start-ups use mostly equity; mature, stable firms carry more debt).
- **External**: market conditions and interest rates, regulation, industry norms.

**Pecking order theory**: because managers know more than investors, firms prefer internal funds, then debt, then (last) new equity. **Signalling**: issuing debt suggests confidence; issuing equity may suggest shares are overvalued.

## Modigliani-Miller propositions
@id modigliani-miller
@r 25
@aka MM propositions; capital structure irrelevance; MM without taxes; MM with taxes; tax shield; cost of equity rises with leverage
@see capital-structure; optimal-capital-structure; wacc

In idealized markets (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, symmetric information):

- **Proposition I (no taxes)**: firm value does not depend on capital structure.
- **Proposition II (no taxes)**: the cost of equity rises linearly with leverage, keeping WACC constant: \(r_e = r_0 + (r_0 - r_d)\frac{D}{E}\).

With corporate taxes, interest is deductible, so debt adds a **tax shield** worth \(t \times D\): \(V_L = V_U + tD\). WACC falls as debt rises. Taken alone this implies 100% debt, which is why bankruptcy costs are added in the trade-off theory.

## Optimal and target capital structure
@id optimal-capital-structure
@r 25
@aka static trade off theory; costs of financial distress; bankruptcy costs; target leverage; optimal debt level
@see modigliani-miller; capital-structure

**Static trade-off theory**: as debt rises, the tax-shield benefit grows but so does the expected cost of financial distress (direct legal costs, lost customers and staff, under-investment). Firm value peaks, and WACC is lowest, at the **optimal capital structure**. Companies set a **target capital structure** near it and let actual weights drift within a range, because issuance has costs and market values move.

## Business models
@id business-models
@r 26
@aka business model; value proposition; subscription model; platform business; network effects; razor and blades; freemium; franchise; asset light; recurring revenue; how a company makes money
@see porters-five-forces; competitive-strategy

A business model describes **who the customers are, what value is offered, how the product is delivered, how it's priced and how the company makes money**. Common types:

- **Subscription / recurring revenue** (software, media).
- **Razor-and-blades**: cheap base product, profitable consumables.
- **Freemium**: free basic tier, paid premium.
- **Platform / marketplace**: connects users; value grows with users (**network effects**).
- **Franchise, licensing, asset-light** models.
- **Bundling, tiered pricing, dynamic pricing**.

Questions for analysis: what are the key revenue drivers and costs, what is unit profitability, how scalable is it, and what risks threaten it?

## Short-term funding alternatives
@id short-term-funding
@r 54, 23
@aka commercial paper; CP; bank lines of credit; revolver; trade credit; factoring; asset based loans; wholesale funding; interbank; certificates of deposit
@see repo; liquidity

Ways corporations fund short-term needs:

- **Bank lines of credit**: uncommitted (bank can refuse), committed/regular, and **revolving** credit agreements (multi-year, can be drawn and repaid).
- **Commercial paper**: short-term unsecured notes for highly rated issuers, usually backed by a bank **backup line** to cover rollover risk.
- **Secured loans** and **factoring** (selling receivables at a discount).
- **Trade credit** from suppliers.

Financial institutions also use retail deposits, certificates of deposit, interbank funds, central-bank funds and **repos**.

## Repurchase agreements (repos)
@id repo
@r 54
@aka repo; reverse repo; repo rate; haircut; margin; tri party repo; secured short term borrowing; collateralized loan
@see short-term-funding

A sale of securities with an agreement to buy them back at a higher price on a set date: effectively a **collateralized loan**. The difference in price is the interest, quoted as the **repo rate**. The lender lends less than the collateral's value; the difference is the **haircut** (margin).

Repo rates are lower for higher-quality, more liquid collateral, shorter terms and when the collateral is in demand ("special"). Risks: counterparty default, collateral value falling, and margin calls. A **reverse repo** is the same trade seen from the cash lender's side.
