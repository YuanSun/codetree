## Breakeven and shutdown points
@id breakeven-shutdown
@r 12
@aka breakeven point; shutdown point; average total cost; average variable cost; when should a firm stop producing; marginal cost; fixed costs sunk
@see economies-of-scale; perfect-competition

A firm **breaks even** when price (average revenue) equals **average total cost** (ATC): it earns zero economic profit. In the short run it should keep operating as long as price covers **average variable cost** (AVC), because fixed costs are paid either way and any revenue above AVC helps pay them. Below AVC, shut down.

| Price vs costs | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| P ≥ ATC | Operate (profit) | Operate |
| AVC ≤ P < ATC | Operate (loss smaller than fixed costs) | Exit |
| P < AVC | Shut down | Exit |

> **At work** Airlines keep flying unprofitable routes in a downturn as long as fares cover fuel and crew (variable costs).

## Economies and diseconomies of scale
@id economies-of-scale
@r 12
@aka returns to scale; long run average cost; LRAC; minimum efficient scale; bigger is cheaper; scale advantages
@see breakeven-shutdown; barriers-to-entry

**Economies of scale**: long-run average cost falls as output grows (specialization, bulk purchasing, spreading fixed costs like R&D). **Diseconomies of scale**: average cost rises beyond some size (bureaucracy, coordination problems). The **minimum efficient scale** is the smallest output at which long-run average cost is at its minimum. Industries with large scale economies tend to be concentrated.

## Perfect competition
@id perfect-competition
@r 12
@aka price taker; many firms; homogeneous product; zero economic profit; commodity market
@see market-structures; breakeven-shutdown

Many firms selling an identical product, free entry and exit, and no pricing power: each firm is a **price taker** facing a horizontal demand curve (P = MR). It produces where **MR = MC**. In the long run entry pushes price to the minimum of ATC, so economic profit is zero. Close examples: agricultural commodities.

## Market structures compared
@id market-structures
@r 12
@aka monopoly; oligopoly; monopolistic competition; perfect competition; pricing power; four market structures; industry structure
@see perfect-competition; monopolistic-competition; oligopoly; monopoly; concentration-measures

| | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sellers | Many | Many | Few | One |
| Product | Identical | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated | Unique, no close substitutes |
| Barriers to entry | None | Low | High | Very high |
| Pricing power | None | Some | Some to significant (interdependent) | Considerable |
| Non-price competition | None | Advertising, quality | Advertising, quality | Advertising |
| Long-run economic profit | Zero | Zero | Can be positive | Positive |

All firms maximize profit at **MR = MC**; structures differ in how price relates to MR.

## Monopolistic competition
@id monopolistic-competition
@r 12
@aka differentiated products; brand; restaurants; downward sloping demand; excess capacity
@see market-structures

Many firms, each with a slightly differentiated product (brand, location, features). Each faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so it has some pricing power, sets output where MR = MC and charges the price demand allows. Easy entry erodes economic profits in the long run, and firms operate with excess capacity (above minimum ATC). Restaurants, clothing brands and hair salons are examples.

## Oligopoly
@id oligopoly
@r 12
@aka few firms; interdependence; kinked demand curve; Cournot; Nash equilibrium; Stackelberg; dominant firm; cartel; collusion; game theory; price war
@see market-structures; concentration-measures

A few large firms whose decisions affect each other. Models of how they price:

- **Kinked demand curve**: rivals match price cuts but not price increases, so prices are sticky.
- **Cournot**: firms choose quantities simultaneously; the result lies between monopoly and competition.
- **Nash equilibrium**: each firm's choice is best given the others'; collusion is tempting but unstable (prisoner's dilemma).
- **Stackelberg**: a leader moves first, followers react.
- **Dominant firm**: one large low-cost firm sets the price; smaller firms take it.

Collusion is more likely with few firms, similar costs, identical products and weak competition law. Examples: aircraft makers, mobile carriers, credit-card networks.

## Monopoly
@id monopoly
@r 12
@aka single seller; price maker; natural monopoly; price discrimination; regulated utility; deadweight loss
@see market-structures; barriers-to-entry

One seller, no close substitutes, high barriers to entry (patents, licences, control of resources, network effects, scale). The monopolist produces where MR = MC and charges a higher price than a competitive market would, creating a deadweight loss.

- **Price discrimination**: charging different prices to groups with different demand elasticities captures more consumer surplus.
- **Natural monopoly**: scale economies so large that one firm serves the market most cheaply (power grids, water). Usually regulated, e.g. price set at average cost.

## Barriers to entry
@id barriers-to-entry
@r 12, 47
@aka moat; competitive advantage; entry barriers; switching costs; network effects; patents; licences; capital requirements
@see porters-five-forces; economies-of-scale; monopoly

Anything that stops new competitors from entering and competing away profits: scale economies, large capital requirements, patents and regulation, brand loyalty, switching costs, control of distribution or resources, and network effects. High barriers support lasting profitability; investors often call them a **moat**.

## Concentration measures (N-firm ratio, HHI)
@id concentration-measures
@r 12, 47
@aka Herfindahl Hirschman index; HHI; concentration ratio; market share; how concentrated is an industry; antitrust
@see market-structures; oligopoly

- **N-firm concentration ratio**: sum of the market shares of the largest N firms. Simple, but it ignores how share is split among them and is not affected by a merger between the top firms.
- **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)**: sum of squared market shares of the largest firms (shares as decimals → 0 to 1, or as percentages → 0 to 10,000). More sensitive to mergers among large firms.

HHI = 1/N for N equal-sized firms, so 1/HHI is the "equivalent number of firms". Neither measure captures the threat of entry or of substitutes, and both depend on how the market is defined.

## Elasticity of demand
@id elasticity
@r 12
@aka price elasticity; income elasticity; cross price elasticity; elastic; inelastic; substitutes; complements; normal good; inferior good
@see monopoly

Responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in something else:

- **Own-price elasticity**: %ΔQ / %ΔP. |e| > 1 elastic (raising price lowers revenue); |e| < 1 inelastic (raising price raises revenue).
- **Income elasticity**: positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods.
- **Cross-price elasticity**: positive for substitutes, negative for complements.

Demand is more elastic when there are close substitutes, the good is a large share of spending, and over longer time horizons.

## Business cycle phases
@id business-cycle
@r 13
@aka recession; expansion; peak; trough; recovery; contraction; economic cycle; output gap; leading indicators
@see credit-cycle; economic-indicators

The fluctuation of economic activity around its long-term trend.

| Phase | Activity | Typical features |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery (early expansion) | Below potential but rising | Inventories rebuilt, hiring resumes slowly, inflation low |
| Expansion | Above trend growth | Rising employment, capex, credit, inflation picks up |
| Slowdown (late cycle) | Near peak, growth slowing | Full employment, inflation and rates high, yield curve flattens |
| Contraction (recession) | Falling | Layoffs, inventory liquidation, falling profits |

> **At work** Cyclical sectors (autos, materials, banks) outperform early in recoveries; defensive sectors (staples, utilities, health care) hold up better in contractions.

## Credit cycle
@id credit-cycle
@r 13
@aka credit boom; lending standards; leverage cycle; credit bust; deleveraging; credit spreads cycle
@see business-cycle; yield-spread-drivers

The expansion and contraction of credit availability. In booms, lenders loosen standards, asset prices rise and borrowers take more leverage; in busts, losses mount, lending tightens and borrowers cut back, deepening the downturn. Credit cycles tend to be longer and deeper than business cycles; recessions that coincide with a credit bust (e.g. housing busts) are usually more severe.

## Economic indicators
@id economic-indicators
@r 13
@aka leading indicators; coincident indicators; lagging indicators; PMI; yield curve inversion; unemployment rate; jobless claims; consumer confidence; housing starts
@see business-cycle

- **Leading** (turn before the economy): average weekly hours in manufacturing, initial jobless claims, new orders, building permits, stock prices, the yield-curve spread, consumer expectations, PMIs.
- **Coincident**: industrial production, real personal income, payroll employment, manufacturing and trade sales.
- **Lagging** (turn after): unemployment duration, inventory-to-sales ratio, unit labour costs, bank prime rate, consumer credit outstanding, services CPI.

Also watch over the cycle: inventories (build late, liquidate early in recession), housing (very rate-sensitive, leads), consumer durables, business capex, and the trade balance (imports rise in strong domestic growth).

## Fiscal policy
@id fiscal-policy
@r 14
@aka government spending; taxes; budget deficit; expansionary fiscal policy; contractionary; stimulus; austerity; automatic stabilizers; fiscal multiplier
@see monetary-policy; national-debt; policy-mix

The government's use of spending and taxes to influence the economy. **Expansionary**: higher spending or lower taxes (bigger deficit). **Contractionary**: the reverse.

- **Automatic stabilizers** (progressive taxes, unemployment benefits) work without new laws: deficits widen in recessions by design. Look at the **structural (cyclically adjusted) balance** to judge the policy stance.
- **Discretionary** policy suffers from lags: recognition, action (legislation) and impact.
- Multiplier: an increase in spending raises GDP by \(1/[1 - MPC(1-t)]\) times the increase; tax cuts have a smaller multiplier.
- Tools: direct taxes (income, wealth), indirect taxes (sales, VAT, excise), transfer payments, current spending, capital spending.

## National debt: does it matter?
@id national-debt
@r 14
@aka debt to GDP; government debt; Ricardian equivalence; crowding out; sovereign debt sustainability
@see fiscal-policy; sovereign-credit

**Reasons for concern**: high debt/GDP may require higher future taxes that discourage work; it may lead to money printing and inflation; borrowing may **crowd out** private investment by raising interest rates.

**Reasons for less concern**: debt owed to the country's own citizens; debt that funds productive investment; **Ricardian equivalence** (people save more today expecting future taxes, offsetting the stimulus); and fiscal deficits may be needed in a recession.

## Monetary policy
@id monetary-policy
@r 15
@aka central bank; interest rate policy; policy rate; open market operations; reserve requirements; quantitative easing; QE; tightening; easing; hawkish; dovish
@see monetary-transmission; central-bank-effectiveness; fiscal-policy; policy-mix

Central bank actions that change the cost and availability of money and credit. Main tools:

- **Policy rate** (e.g. the rate the central bank charges banks or pays on reserves).
- **Open market operations**: buying government securities adds reserves (expansionary); selling drains them.
- **Reserve requirements**: lower requirements let banks lend more.
- **Quantitative easing**: large-scale asset purchases when the policy rate is near zero, to push down longer-term yields.

Objectives: price stability (usually an inflation target near 2%), often also maximum employment, a stable currency and financial stability.

> **At work** "Hawkish" means leaning toward tighter policy to fight inflation; "dovish" means leaning toward easier policy to support growth.

## Monetary transmission mechanism
@id monetary-transmission
@r 15
@aka how interest rates affect the economy; neutral rate; transmission channels; liquidity trap; real interest rate
@see monetary-policy

How a change in the policy rate spreads to the economy: it moves **market interest rates**, **asset prices** (bonds, stocks, houses), **expectations and confidence**, and the **exchange rate**. These change consumption, investment and net exports, then output and inflation, with long and variable lags.

The **neutral rate** is the policy rate that neither stimulates nor restrains: roughly the real trend growth rate plus the inflation target. Policy above neutral is contractionary. Limits: in a **liquidity trap** people hoard money and lower rates do little; banks may not lend extra reserves.

## Qualities of effective central banks; targeting regimes
@id central-bank-effectiveness
@r 15
@aka central bank independence; credibility; transparency; inflation targeting; exchange rate targeting; currency peg; forward guidance
@see monetary-policy; exchange-rate-regimes

Effective central banks are **independent** (operationally and in setting targets), **credible** (people believe they will hit the target) and **transparent** (they explain decisions, e.g. inflation reports, forward guidance). Credibility anchors inflation expectations, which makes the job easier.

- **Inflation targeting**: most common; a public target (e.g. 2% CPI) with a tolerance band.
- **Exchange rate targeting**: the central bank buys or sells its currency to hold a peg; it gives up independent monetary policy and imports the anchor country's inflation.

## Geopolitics: cooperation vs. competition
@id geopolitics
@r 16
@aka geopolitics; state actors; non state actors; globalization; nationalism; hegemony; autarky; multilateralism; bilateralism; soft power; hard power
@see geopolitical-risk; international-organizations

How countries' geography, resources and politics shape their relations. Countries range from **cooperative** (shared rules, trade, alliances) to **non-cooperative** (self-reliance, competition). Combining this with attitudes to **globalization** gives four archetypes:

| | Pro-globalization | Anti-globalization |
|---|---|---|
| Cooperative | Multilateralism | Regionalism / bilateralism |
| Non-cooperative | Hegemony | Autarky |

Tools of geopolitics: **national security** (armed conflict, espionage, alliances), **economic** (trade agreements, sanctions, tariffs, cartels like OPEC) and **financial** (currency reserves, capital controls, investment screening, access to payment systems).

## Geopolitical risk
@id geopolitical-risk
@r 16
@aka political risk; event risk; exogenous risk; thematic risk; sanctions risk; war risk; scenario analysis geopolitics
@see geopolitics

The risk that tensions between states, or within them, affect economies and markets. Types: **event risk** (elections, known dates), **exogenous risk** (sudden: coups, invasions), **thematic risk** (slow-burning trends: climate migration, cyber, tech rivalry). Assess its **likelihood**, **velocity** (how fast it hits markets) and **size and breadth** of impact. Investors use scenario analysis, stress tests and signposts, and may diversify across countries or hedge with safe-haven assets.

## International organizations: IMF, World Bank, WTO
@id international-organizations
@r 16
@aka IMF; International Monetary Fund; World Bank; WTO; World Trade Organization; Bretton Woods
@see international-trade; geopolitics

- **IMF**: promotes monetary cooperation and exchange-rate stability; lends to countries with balance-of-payments problems, usually with policy conditions; monitors economies.
- **World Bank**: long-term financing and technical help for development and poverty reduction (infrastructure, education, health).
- **WTO**: sets and enforces the rules of international trade; resolves trade disputes; negotiates trade liberalization.

## Gains from international trade
@id international-trade
@r 17
@aka comparative advantage; absolute advantage; free trade; benefits of trade; costs of trade; Ricardian model; Heckscher Ohlin
@see trade-restrictions; trading-blocs

Countries gain by specializing in what they produce at the lowest **opportunity cost** (**comparative advantage**), even if another country is better at everything (absolute advantage). Benefits: lower prices and more choice, economies of scale, more competition, technology transfer. Costs: job losses and wage pressure in industries facing imports, and dependence on foreign suppliers.

Sources of comparative advantage: productivity differences (Ricardian model) and differences in factor endowments, e.g. capital vs labour (Heckscher-Ohlin model).

## Trade restrictions: tariffs, quotas, subsidies
@id trade-restrictions
@r 17
@aka tariff; import quota; export subsidy; voluntary export restraint; protectionism; trade war; deadweight loss of tariff
@see international-trade

| Tool | What it is | Who gains |
|---|---|---|
| Tariff | Tax on imports | Domestic producers, government (revenue) |
| Import quota | Limit on import quantity | Domestic producers; foreign exporters with licences (quota rents) |
| Voluntary export restraint | Exporting country limits its own exports | Foreign exporters capture the higher price |
| Export subsidy | Payment to exporters | Exporters; domestic consumers pay higher prices |

All of them raise domestic prices, reduce consumer surplus and create deadweight losses for the world as a whole. Large countries imposing tariffs may improve their terms of trade.

## Trading blocs, common markets and economic unions
@id trading-blocs
@r 17
@aka free trade area; customs union; common market; economic union; monetary union; regional trade agreement; eurozone
@see international-trade

Levels of integration, each adding to the previous:

1. **Free trade area**: no barriers between members.
2. **Customs union**: + common external trade policy.
3. **Common market**: + free movement of labour and capital.
4. **Economic union**: + common economic institutions and policies.
5. **Monetary union**: + single currency (e.g. the eurozone).

Benefits: larger markets, scale economies, competition, investment and cooperation. Costs: trade diversion away from efficient non-members, loss of policy independence, and adjustment pains for some industries.

## Foreign exchange market basics
@id fx-market
@r 18, 19
@aka FX; foreign exchange; currency quote; base currency; price currency; spot rate; exchange rate quote; appreciation; depreciation; FX market participants
@see real-exchange-rate; cross-rate; forward-exchange-rate

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. In a quote **A/B = 1.25**, A is the **price** currency and B the **base** currency: 1 unit of B costs 1.25 units of A. (Market convention like EUR/USD often writes the base first; be sure which way a quote reads.)

If the rate rises, the base currency has **appreciated** against the price currency. Percentage change in the base currency = new/old − 1; the price currency moves by old/new − 1 (not the same number).

Participants: corporations hedging trade flows, investors, central banks and sovereign funds, governments, banks and dealers, retail traders. It is the largest and most liquid financial market, mostly over the counter.

## Real exchange rate
@id real-exchange-rate
@r 18
@aka real vs nominal exchange rate; purchasing power; competitiveness; inflation adjusted exchange rate
@see fx-market

The nominal rate adjusted for price levels: it measures the relative purchasing power of two currencies.

$$\text{real}_{d/f} = S_{d/f}\times\frac{CPI_f}{CPI_d}$$

If domestic inflation is higher than foreign inflation and the nominal rate does not adjust, the real value of the domestic currency rises and its exports become less competitive.

## Exchange rate regimes
@id exchange-rate-regimes
@r 18
@aka currency peg; fixed exchange rate; floating exchange rate; managed float; currency board; dollarization; crawling peg; target zone
@see central-bank-effectiveness; fx-market

From most to least rigid:

- **No separate legal tender** (formal dollarization; monetary union members).
- **Currency board**: commitment to exchange domestic currency for a foreign one at a fixed rate, fully backed by reserves (Hong Kong).
- **Conventional fixed peg**: fixed to a currency or basket, within a narrow band.
- **Target zone**: fixed parity with a wider band.
- **Crawling peg**: parity adjusted periodically (e.g. for inflation).
- **Managed float**: central bank intervenes without a target.
- **Independently floating**: the market sets the rate.

The "impossible trinity": a country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free capital flows and independent monetary policy all at once.

## Exchange rates, trade balance and capital flows
@id exchange-rate-trade
@r 18
@aka current account; capital account; trade deficit; balance of payments; elasticities approach; Marshall Lerner; J curve; absorption approach
@see exchange-rate-regimes; capital-restrictions

A country's current account deficit must be matched by a capital (financial) account surplus: it borrows or sells assets abroad. A **depreciation** makes exports cheaper and imports dearer; it improves the trade balance if demand is elastic enough (**Marshall-Lerner condition**). The improvement often comes after an initial worsening (the **J-curve**), because contracts and volumes adjust slowly. The absorption view adds that the trade balance only improves if national saving rises relative to investment.

## Capital restrictions
@id capital-restrictions
@r 18
@aka capital controls; foreign ownership limits; restrictions on capital flows; repatriation restrictions
@see exchange-rate-regimes

Governments restrict capital flows to: reduce the volatility of domestic asset prices, keep an exchange-rate target while running independent monetary policy, keep domestic savings at home to fund local investment, protect strategic industries from foreign ownership, and stop "hot money" from leaving in a crisis. Costs: less investment, less market efficiency, and possible evasion.

## Cross rate
@id cross-rate
@r 19
@aka currency cross rate; triangular arbitrage; EUR JPY via USD; implied exchange rate
@see fx-market

The exchange rate between two currencies implied by their rates against a third currency (often USD). Multiply the quotes so the common currency cancels:

$$\frac{EUR}{JPY}\ \text{via}\ \frac{EUR}{USD}\times\frac{USD}{JPY}$$

(write each quote as price/base and check that the middle currency cancels). If a dealer's quoted cross rate differs from the implied one, **triangular arbitrage** is possible.

## Forward exchange rate and covered interest parity
@id forward-exchange-rate
@r 19
@aka covered interest rate parity; forward points; forward premium; forward discount; FX forward; currency hedge
@see fx-market; forward-pricing

No-arbitrage forward rate: investing at home must earn the same as converting, investing abroad and converting back at a locked-in forward rate.

$$F_{f/d} = S_{f/d}\times\frac{1 + i_f\,(\tfrac{days}{360})}{1 + i_d\,(\tfrac{days}{360})}$$

(with f = price currency, d = base currency). The currency with the **higher interest rate trades at a forward discount**. **Forward points** = (F − S) quoted in pips (usually ×10,000); add them to the spot rate to get the forward.

> **At work** Hedging foreign bonds with forwards earns (or costs) roughly the interest-rate differential, which is why hedged foreign yields can look very different from unhedged ones.
