## Bond features
@id bond-features
@r 51
@aka bond basics; issuer; maturity; par value; face value; coupon rate; currency denomination; seniority; tenor; perpetual bond; zero coupon bond
@see bond-indenture; cash-flow-structures

The basic terms of a bond:

- **Issuer**: governments, agencies, supranationals, companies, special-purpose entities.
- **Maturity / tenor**: when principal is repaid (money market ≤ 1 year; perpetual bonds never mature).
- **Par (face) value**: the principal repaid.
- **Coupon rate**: annual interest as a % of par; paid annually, semi-annually or quarterly. A **zero-coupon** bond pays only par at maturity and is sold at a discount.
- **Currency**: dual-currency bonds pay coupons in one currency and principal in another.
- **Seniority** and **security** (collateral).

## Bond indenture and covenants
@id bond-indenture
@r 51
@aka indenture; trust deed; bond covenants; affirmative covenants; negative covenants; trustee; collateral; secured vs unsecured; restrictive covenants
@see bond-features; seniority

The **indenture** (trust deed) is the legal contract between issuer and bondholders, enforced by a trustee. It sets out the terms, the collateral and **covenants**:

- **Affirmative**: what the issuer must do (pay on time, maintain assets and insurance, comply with laws, report).
- **Negative**: what it must not do (limits on extra debt, asset sales, liens on assets (negative pledge), dividends and buybacks, mergers).

Covenants protect lenders but cost the issuer flexibility. High-yield bonds have stronger covenants than investment-grade ones.

## Cash flow structures
@id cash-flow-structures
@r 52
@aka bullet bond; amortizing bond; fully amortizing; partially amortizing; balloon payment; sinking fund; floating rate note; FRN; step up coupon; payment in kind; PIK; deferred coupon; inflation linked bond; index linked
@see bond-features; frn; embedded-options

Ways principal and interest are paid:

- **Bullet**: coupons, then all principal at maturity.
- **Amortizing**: principal repaid over time (fully, or partially with a **balloon** at the end). **Sinking funds** retire part of the issue each year.
- **Floating-rate notes**: coupon = market reference rate + a fixed spread, reset periodically; can have caps and floors.
- **Step-up coupons**: rise on schedule (or after a downgrade).
- **Credit-linked coupons**: change with the issuer's rating.
- **Payment-in-kind (PIK)**: interest paid with more bonds.
- **Deferred coupons**: no coupons at first.
- **Index-linked (inflation-linked)** bonds: coupons and/or principal adjust with an index such as CPI.

## Embedded options: callable, putable, convertible
@id embedded-options
@r 52, 63
@aka callable bond; call option; call protection; make whole call; putable bond; put option; convertible bond; conversion ratio; conversion price; contingent convertible; CoCo; option adjusted
@see effective-duration; negative-convexity

Contingency provisions in the bond:

- **Callable**: the **issuer** can redeem early (usually when rates fall). Benefits the issuer, so it trades at a higher yield / lower price. Call protection, call schedules and **make-whole** calls (redeem at PV of remaining payments at a small spread) limit this.
- **Putable**: the **investor** can sell back to the issuer at a set price (useful when rates rise or credit worsens). Lower yield / higher price.
- **Convertible**: the investor can exchange the bond for a set number of shares (**conversion ratio**). Conversion value = ratio × share price. Lower coupon in exchange for equity upside.
- **Contingent convertibles (CoCos)**: convert automatically (or are written down) if the issuer's capital falls below a trigger; used by banks.

Value of a callable bond = straight bond − call option; putable = straight bond + put option.

## Legal, regulatory and tax considerations
@id bond-legal-tax
@r 52
@aka domestic bond; foreign bond; eurobond; global bond; bearer bonds; registered bonds; withholding tax; tax exempt municipal bonds; original issue discount tax
@see bond-features

- **Domestic bonds**: issued by a local issuer in the local market and currency.
- **Foreign bonds**: issued by a foreign issuer in another country's domestic market.
- **Eurobonds**: issued outside the jurisdiction of the currency's country; less regulated, usually bearer form historically.
- **Global bonds**: issued in the Eurobond market and at least one domestic market at once.

Tax features: interest usually taxed as income; some government or municipal bonds are tax-exempt; capital gains may be taxed differently; original-issue-discount bonds may be taxed on accreted interest each year.

## Fixed-income market segments and participants
@id fi-market-segments
@r 53
@aka bond market segments; investment grade vs high yield; sovereign bonds; corporate bonds; money market; developed vs emerging; bond investors; who buys bonds
@see fi-indexes; fi-primary-secondary

Bonds are classified by **issuer** (household-backed securitizations, non-financial corporates, financials, governments), **credit quality** (investment grade BBB−/Baa3 and above vs high yield), **maturity** (money market vs capital market), **currency**, **geography** (developed, emerging) and features (green bonds, inflation-linked).

Investors include central banks, institutions (pensions, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, banks), mutual funds and ETFs, and retail investors. Banks and dealers make markets.

## Fixed-income indexes
@id fi-indexes
@r 53
@aka bond index; aggregate bond index; bond benchmark; index replication; stratified sampling bonds; bond ETF
@see fi-market-segments; index-weighting

Bond indexes are usually market-value weighted and grouped by segment (aggregate, government, corporate IG, high yield, EM, inflation-linked). They are hard to replicate: thousands of bonds, many illiquid, constantly maturing and being issued. Index funds use **stratified sampling** to match key exposures (duration, sector, quality) with fewer bonds. Market-value weighting means the most indebted issuers get the largest weights.

## Bond primary and secondary markets
@id fi-primary-secondary
@r 53
@aka bond issuance; auction; underwritten offering; private placement; OTC bond trading; dealer market; bid ask spread bonds; shelf registration
@see fi-market-segments; government-issuance

**Primary**: governments typically sell by **auction**; companies by underwritten (bought deal) or best-efforts offerings, shelf registrations, or private placements. **Secondary**: mostly **over-the-counter dealer markets**, less transparent and less liquid than equity exchanges, with wider bid-ask spreads for older ("off-the-run") or lower-rated bonds. Electronic trading has grown.

## Corporate long-term funding: investment grade vs. high yield
@id corporate-funding
@r 54
@aka investment grade issuers; high yield issuers; junk bonds; leveraged loans; covenant lite; bank loans vs bonds; maturity profile; secured debt high yield
@see credit-ratings; corporate-credit-analysis

**Investment-grade** issuers fund mostly with unsecured bonds across many maturities, at low spreads and with light covenants; they use bank lines for liquidity. **High-yield** issuers rely more on **secured bank loans and leveraged loans**, issue bonds with shorter maturities, call features and stronger covenants, and their debt structures have several layers of seniority. Their access to markets is more cyclical.

## Government and non-sovereign issuers
@id government-issuance
@r 55
@aka sovereign bonds; treasury bills; government auctions; on the run; off the run; agency bonds; quasi government; supranational; municipal bonds; general obligation; revenue bonds; regional government
@see sovereign-credit; fi-primary-secondary

- **Sovereign governments** fund deficits with bills, notes and bonds (and inflation-linked bonds), sold by regular **auctions**. The most recently issued bond of a maturity is **on-the-run** (most liquid, used as the benchmark).
- **Non-sovereign governments** (states, provinces, cities): **general obligation** bonds backed by taxing power, and **revenue bonds** backed by a specific project's income (riskier).
- **Agencies / quasi-government entities**: government-sponsored, often with implicit or explicit guarantees.
- **Supranationals** (World Bank, regional development banks): high-rated, fund development lending.

## Bond pricing with a yield to maturity
@id bond-pricing
@r 56
@aka bond price formula; price yield relationship; discount bond; premium bond; par bond; pricing between coupon dates; full price; flat price; accrued interest; clean price; dirty price
@see ytm; accrued-interest; matrix-pricing

A bond's price is the PV of its cash flows discounted at its yield to maturity (per period r):

$$P = \sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{C}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+r)^N}$$

Relationships:

- Price and yield move **inversely**.
- Coupon < yield → **discount**; coupon = yield → **par**; coupon > yield → **premium**.
- Lower coupons and longer maturities → bigger % price change for a given yield change.
- Convexity: price rises more when yields fall than it drops when yields rise by the same amount.
- **Constant-yield price trajectory**: a premium or discount bond's price converges to par as maturity approaches ("pull to par").

## Accrued interest, full and flat price
@id accrued-interest
@r 56
@aka accrued interest; full price; dirty price; invoice price; flat price; clean price; quoted price; day count convention; actual actual; 30/360
@see bond-pricing

Between coupon dates the buyer pays the seller for the interest earned so far.

$$P_{full} = P_0(1+r)^{t/T} \qquad AI = \frac{t}{T}\times PMT \qquad P_{flat} = P_{full} - AI$$

(t = days since the last coupon, T = days in the coupon period). Prices are **quoted flat (clean)** so they don't jump on coupon dates; settlement is at the **full (dirty)** price. Day counts: **actual/actual** for most government bonds, **30/360** for many corporates.

## Matrix pricing
@id matrix-pricing
@r 56
@aka matrix pricing; pricing illiquid bonds; interpolating yields; comparable bond yields; estimated yield spread
@see bond-pricing; yield-spreads

Estimating the yield (and price) of a bond that rarely trades from the yields of **comparable** liquid bonds (same rating and sector, similar maturity), usually by linear interpolation by maturity. Also used to set the yield on a new issue. It's an estimate: adjust for differences in liquidity, features and seniority.

## Yield to maturity (YTM)
@id ytm
@r 56, 57
@aka YTM; yield to maturity; internal rate of return of a bond; redemption yield; market yield; bond yield
@see bond-pricing; yield-measures; current-yield

The single discount rate that makes the PV of a bond's promised cash flows equal its price: the bond's [[irr]]. YTM is the realized return only if the investor holds to maturity, every payment is made on time, and all coupons are reinvested at the YTM.

> **At work** "The 10-year is at 4.2%" refers to the YTM of the benchmark 10-year government bond.

## Yield measures and compounding conventions
@id yield-measures
@r 57
@aka annual yield; semiannual bond basis; street convention; true yield; yield to call; yield to worst; current yield; simple yield; periodicity; bond equivalent yield; converting yields
@see ytm; current-yield; ear

- **Periodicity**: a yield quoted for semi-annual compounding (bond-equivalent yield) differs from an annual-pay yield. Convert: \((1 + y_m/m)^m = (1 + y_n/n)^n\).
- **Street convention** assumes payments on scheduled dates; **true yield** uses actual dates (weekends, holidays).
- **Current yield**: annual coupon / flat price.
- **Simple yield**: (coupon + straight-line amortization of discount or premium) / flat price.
- **Yield to call** (to each call date) and **yield to worst** (the lowest of YTM and all yields to call).

## Current yield
@id current-yield
@r 57
@aka current yield; running yield; income yield; coupon over price
@see yield-measures

Annual coupon / flat price. It ignores the gain or loss from pull-to-par and the time value of money, so it's a rough measure of income only.

## Yield spreads (fixed-rate bonds)
@id yield-spreads
@r 57
@aka yield spread; benchmark spread; G spread; I spread; Z spread; zero volatility spread; option adjusted spread; OAS; credit spread; spread over treasuries
@see yield-spread-drivers; spot-rate; credit-risk

Measures of the extra yield over a benchmark:

- **Benchmark spread**: YTM − yield of the on-the-run government bond of similar maturity.
- **G-spread**: spread over an (interpolated) government yield of the same maturity.
- **I-spread**: spread over the swap rate of the same maturity.
- **Z-spread**: the constant spread added to every point on the government **spot** curve that prices the bond; more precise for non-flat curves.
- **Option-adjusted spread (OAS)**: Z-spread − option cost. Used for bonds with embedded options.

Spreads reflect credit risk, liquidity risk and (unless removed) option risk; the benchmark yield reflects macro factors (real rates and inflation).

## Floating-rate notes: quoted and discount margin
@id frn
@r 58
@aka floating rate note; FRN; floater; quoted margin; discount margin; required margin; market reference rate; MRR; SOFR; reset; inverse floater
@see cash-flow-structures; money-market-yields

An FRN pays MRR (e.g. SOFR) + a **quoted margin** fixed at issue. The **discount (required) margin** is the spread the market now requires.

- Quoted margin = discount margin → priced at par on reset dates.
- Credit worsens → discount margin > quoted margin → price below par.

Because coupons reset to market rates, FRNs have very little interest-rate risk (duration ≈ time to the next reset); their price risk comes mostly from changes in credit spreads.

## Money market yields
@id money-market-yields
@r 58
@aka discount rate basis; add on rate; bank discount yield; treasury bill yield; money market yield; CD yield; bond equivalent yield; 360 vs 365 day
@see frn; yield-measures

Short-term instruments quote rates in two ways:

- **Discount rate** (T-bills, commercial paper): \(PV = FV(1 - \frac{days}{year} \times DR)\). Understates the return because it's a % of face value.
- **Add-on rate** (CDs, repos, interbank loans): \(FV = PV(1 + \frac{days}{year} \times AOR)\).

Year conventions differ (360 or 365 days). To compare, convert both to an add-on rate on a 365-day basis (**bond-equivalent yield**).

## Spot rates and the spot curve
@id spot-rate
@r 59
@aka spot rate; zero coupon rate; zero curve; spot curve; pricing bond with spot rates; bootstrapping spot rates; term structure
@see par-rate; forward-rate; yield-curve-shapes

A **spot rate** is the yield on a zero-coupon bond for a given maturity. The **spot curve (zero curve)** plots them. Each cash flow should be discounted at the spot rate for its own date:

$$P = \frac{C}{(1+S_1)} + \frac{C}{(1+S_2)^2} + \cdots + \frac{C+F}{(1+S_N)^N}$$

Spot rates are derived from coupon-bond prices by **bootstrapping**: solve for S₁ from the 1-year bond, then S₂ from the 2-year bond using S₁, and so on.

## Par rates and the par curve
@id par-rate
@r 59
@aka par curve; par yield; par rate; coupon that prices at par; swap curve; benchmark yield curve
@see spot-rate; forward-rate

The **par rate** for maturity N is the coupon rate that makes an N-year bond price at par given the spot curve:

$$1 = \frac{PMT}{1+S_1} + \frac{PMT}{(1+S_2)^2} + \cdots + \frac{PMT + 1}{(1+S_N)^N}$$

The **par curve** is what's usually shown as "the" government yield curve, since on-the-run bonds trade near par.

## Forward rates
@id forward-rate
@r 59, 74
@aka forward rate; implied forward rate; 1y1y; 2y1y; forward curve; break even reinvestment rate; future interest rate; FRA rate
@see spot-rate; cash-flow-additivity; fra

A **forward rate** is a rate agreed today for borrowing or lending over a future period. "2y1y" is the 1-year rate starting 2 years from now. By no-arbitrage:

$$(1+S_3)^3 = (1+S_2)^2(1+F_{2,1})$$

Forward rates are the breakeven reinvestment rates: if future rates turn out equal to today's forwards, all strategies earn the same. If the spot curve slopes up, the forward curve lies above it.

## Shapes of the yield curve
@id yield-curve-shapes
@r 59, 13
@aka upward sloping yield curve; inverted yield curve; flat yield curve; humped; normal yield curve; term structure; yield curve inversion recession; spot vs par vs forward curves
@see spot-rate; par-rate; forward-rate; business-cycle

- **Upward-sloping (normal)**: long rates above short rates (expected rises in rates and/or term premiums). Forward curve > spot curve > par curve.
- **Inverted**: short rates above long rates; markets expect rates to fall. Often precedes recessions. Forward < spot < par.
- **Flat** and **humped** curves also occur.

## Sources of return on a bond
@id sources-of-bond-return
@r 60
@aka bond return components; coupon income; reinvestment income; capital gain or loss; rolldown; horizon return; reinvestment risk; market price risk
@see macaulay-duration; ytm

A fixed-rate bond's return comes from: (1) coupon payments, (2) **reinvestment** income on those coupons, (3) capital gain or loss when sold (including pull-to-par).

Two opposing risks when rates change: higher rates **lower the price** (market price risk) but **raise reinvestment income** (reinvestment risk works the other way). Over a short horizon price risk dominates; over a long horizon reinvestment dominates.

## Macaulay duration
@id macaulay-duration
@r 60
@aka Macaulay duration; weighted average time to cash flows; investment horizon; immunization; duration gap; holding period and duration
@see modified-duration; sources-of-bond-return

The weighted average time until a bond's cash flows are received, with weights equal to each cash flow's share of the bond's price.

$$MacDur = \sum_{t=1}^{N} t\times\frac{PV(CF_t)}{P}$$

For a single parallel shift in rates right after purchase, an investor whose **horizon equals the Macaulay duration** is roughly hedged: price and reinvestment effects offset (the basis of **immunization**). **Duration gap** = Macaulay duration − investment horizon: positive means exposure to rising rates; negative means exposure to falling rates. A zero-coupon bond's Macaulay duration equals its maturity.

## Modified duration
@id modified-duration
@r 61
@aka duration; modified duration; ModDur; price sensitivity to yield; interest rate sensitivity; percentage price change for 1% yield change; approximate modified duration; bond risk
@see macaulay-duration; money-duration; convexity; effective-duration

The approximate percentage change in a bond's price for a 1% (100 bp) change in its yield.

$$ModDur = \frac{MacDur}{1+r} \qquad \%\Delta P \approx -ModDur\times\Delta y$$

Approximation from prices: \(ApproxModDur = \frac{PV_- - PV_+}{2\,\Delta y\,PV_0}\).

> **Example** ModDur = 7.2. If yields rise 50 bp, the price falls about 7.2 × 0.5% = 3.6%.

> **At work** "Duration" on a bond fund fact sheet is almost always modified or effective duration: a fund with duration 6 loses about 6% if rates rise 1%.

## Money duration and PVBP (DV01)
@id money-duration
@r 61
@aka money duration; dollar duration; PVBP; price value of a basis point; DV01; BPV; basis point value; risk in dollars
@see modified-duration

Duration in currency units rather than percent:

$$\text{MoneyDur} = ModDur\times P_{full} \qquad \Delta P \approx -\text{MoneyDur}\times\Delta y$$

**PVBP (DV01)** = the change in value for a 1 bp change in yield: \(\frac{PV_- - PV_+}{2}\) with ±1 bp. Traders size hedges with it: hedge ratio = DV01 of position / DV01 of hedge instrument.

## Drivers of interest rate risk
@id duration-drivers
@r 61
@aka what affects duration; maturity and duration; coupon and duration; yield level and duration; longer bonds riskier
@see modified-duration; macaulay-duration

All else equal, duration (interest rate risk) is **higher** when:

- **Maturity is longer** (usually; deep-discount long bonds can be exceptions).
- **Coupon is lower** (more value comes at the end; zero-coupon bonds have the highest).
- **Yield is lower**.

Callable bonds have lower duration than straight bonds when rates fall (the call caps price gains); FRNs have very low rate duration.

## Convexity
@id convexity
@r 62
@aka convexity; convexity adjustment; second order effect; curvature; positive convexity; duration and convexity approximation; approximate convexity
@see modified-duration; negative-convexity; effective-convexity

Duration is a straight-line approximation; the true price-yield curve is **convex**. Adding the convexity term improves the estimate:

$$\%\Delta P \approx -ModDur\,\Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2}\,Convexity\,(\Delta y)^2$$

Approximate convexity from prices: \(\frac{PV_- + PV_+ - 2PV_0}{(\Delta y)^2 PV_0}\).

For option-free bonds convexity is positive: price rises more when yields fall than it falls when yields rise. Convexity is higher for longer maturities, lower coupons and lower yields, and for more dispersed cash flows. Investors pay for it (convexity is valuable when rates are volatile).

> **Example** ModDur 7, convexity 60, yields +100 bp: −7% + ½ × 60 × 0.0001 = −7% + 0.30% = −6.7%.

## Portfolio duration and convexity
@id portfolio-duration
@r 62
@aka portfolio duration; weighted average duration; portfolio convexity; parallel shift assumption; limitations of duration
@see modified-duration; key-rate-duration

Portfolio duration ≈ market-value-weighted average of the bonds' durations (similarly for convexity). Simple and widely used, but it **assumes parallel shifts**: every yield changes by the same amount. In reality curves twist and bend, so use [[key-rate-duration]] for non-parallel moves. (The more exact approach uses the portfolio's cash-flow yield, which is rarely done in practice.)

## Effective duration and convexity (bonds with options)
@id effective-duration
@r 63
@aka effective duration; option adjusted duration; curve duration; benchmark yield curve shift; duration of callable bonds; MBS duration
@see effective-convexity; embedded-options; modified-duration

When cash flows change with rates (callable, putable bonds, MBS), yield-based duration doesn't work. **Effective duration** measures sensitivity to a parallel shift in the **benchmark curve**, using a valuation model to reprice the bond:

$$EffDur = \frac{PV_- - PV_+}{2\,\Delta\text{Curve}\,PV_0} \qquad EffCon = \frac{PV_- + PV_+ - 2PV_0}{(\Delta\text{Curve})^2 PV_0}$$

It's the right measure for bonds with embedded options. For an option-free bond it's close to modified duration.

## Negative convexity
@id negative-convexity
@r 63
@aka negative convexity; callable bond price compression; MBS negative convexity; price capped when rates fall; putable bond convexity
@see embedded-options; effective-duration; prepayment-risk

Callable bonds and mortgage securities can have **negative convexity** at low yields: as rates fall the issuer is likely to call (or homeowners refinance), so the price rises less than it falls for equal moves. Putable bonds have more positive convexity at high yields, since the put cushions losses.

## Effective convexity
@id effective-convexity
@r 63
@aka effective convexity; curve convexity
@see effective-duration; convexity

Convexity measured against shifts in the benchmark curve using a pricing model, analogous to [[effective-duration]]. Negative for callable bonds when the call is near the money.

## Key rate duration
@id key-rate-duration
@r 63
@aka key rate duration; partial duration; yield curve risk; non parallel shift; twist; steepening; flattening; butterfly
@see portfolio-duration; effective-duration

The sensitivity of a bond or portfolio to a change in **one** maturity point on the benchmark curve (e.g. the 2-, 5-, 10- and 30-year rates), holding the others constant. Key rate durations add up to the effective duration. They show exposure to curve **steepening, flattening and twists**, which a single duration number hides.

## Empirical vs. analytical duration
@id empirical-duration
@r 63
@aka empirical duration; analytical duration; regression duration; observed duration; high yield bond rate sensitivity; spread duration
@see effective-duration

**Analytical** durations come from formulas or models assuming yields change by the stated amount. **Empirical** duration is estimated by regressing a bond's actual price changes on changes in benchmark yields. They differ when spreads move with rates: for high-yield and other credit-risky bonds, falling government yields often come with **widening** credit spreads (risk-off), so empirical duration is lower than analytical. For government bonds the two are close.

## Credit risk: PD and LGD
@id credit-risk
@r 64
@aka credit risk; default risk; probability of default; PD; loss given default; LGD; recovery rate; expected loss; credit spread risk; downgrade risk
@see credit-ratings; yield-spread-drivers; seniority

The risk of loss because a borrower doesn't pay as promised.

$$\text{Expected loss} = PD \times LGD, \qquad LGD = (1 - \text{recovery rate})\times\text{exposure}$$

Related risks: **credit migration (downgrade) risk** and **spread risk** (prices fall when spreads widen, even without default). Roughly, credit spread ≈ PD × LGD (plus a liquidity and risk premium).

## Credit ratings and their limits
@id credit-ratings
@r 64
@aka credit rating agencies; Moody's; S&P; Fitch; investment grade; high yield; junk; BBB minus; Baa3; issuer rating; issue rating; notching; fallen angel; rating outlook
@see credit-risk; seniority

Ratings rank relative default risk. Investment grade is BBB−/Baa3 and above; below that is **high yield (speculative)**. Agencies rate the **issuer** (corporate family rating, usually for senior unsecured debt) and each **issue**; issues are **notched** up or down from the issuer rating by seniority and security (more notching for lower-rated issuers).

Uses: regulation and mandates (many funds may hold only investment grade), pricing, covenants. Limits: ratings change slowly and lag the market; agencies make mistakes; event risks (lawsuits, LBOs, disasters) are hard to rate; issuer-paid conflicts of interest. A **fallen angel** is a bond downgraded from investment grade to high yield.

## Factors driving yield spreads
@id yield-spread-drivers
@r 64
@aka what drives credit spreads; spread widening; spread tightening; risk on risk off; credit cycle spreads; liquidity premium; spread volatility
@see yield-spreads; credit-cycle; credit-risk

- **Macroeconomic**: spreads widen in recessions and narrow in expansions; rising defaults, falling profits and tighter credit widen them.
- **Market**: liquidity and dealer capacity; flows into or out of credit funds; supply of new issues.
- **Issuer-specific**: leverage, coverage, profitability, events (M&A, buybacks), rating changes.

Lower-rated bonds have wider and more volatile spreads, and their spreads react more to the economy.

## Sovereign and non-sovereign government credit
@id sovereign-credit
@r 65
@aka sovereign credit analysis; sovereign rating; ability and willingness to pay; local currency debt; foreign currency debt; institutional assessment; fiscal flexibility; external position; municipal credit
@see government-issuance; national-debt

Sovereigns are judged on both **ability and willingness** to pay:

- **Institutional and economic**: rule of law, political stability, effective policy-making, growth prospects, income per capita, diversification.
- **Fiscal**: debt/GDP, interest burden, ability to raise taxes or cut spending.
- **Monetary and external**: credible central bank, exchange-rate regime, reserve currency status, foreign-currency debt, current account, international reserves.

Local-currency debt is usually rated higher than foreign-currency debt, since the sovereign can tax and print its own currency. Non-sovereign governments: general obligation bonds depend on the tax base and budget; revenue bonds on the project's cash flows and **debt service coverage**.

## Corporate credit analysis: qualitative factors (4 Cs)
@id corporate-credit-analysis
@r 66
@aka four Cs of credit; capacity; capital; collateral; covenants; character; credit analysis; business risk; industry risk; management quality
@see credit-ratios; seniority; bond-indenture

A traditional framework, the **Cs of credit**:

- **Capacity**: ability to pay from cash flows; industry structure, competitive position, operating history, cyclicality.
- **Capital**: other resources and leverage; how much equity cushions creditors.
- **Collateral**: assets securing the debt and their value in default.
- **Covenants**: terms protecting lenders.
- **Character**: management's integrity, strategy, track record and accounting conservatism.

## Seniority ranking and priority of claims
@id seniority
@r 66
@aka priority of claims; capital structure ranking; senior secured; first lien; second lien; senior unsecured; subordinated; junior debt; pari passu; absolute priority rule; recovery rates by seniority; structural subordination
@see credit-risk; credit-ratings

In bankruptcy, claims are generally paid in order:

1. First lien / senior secured
2. Second lien / secured
3. Senior unsecured
4. Senior subordinated
5. Subordinated
6. Junior subordinated
7. Preferred stock
8. Common equity

Claims of the same rank are **pari passu**. Higher seniority → higher expected recovery → higher issue rating via notching. In practice (especially in reorganizations), the **absolute priority rule** is often bent: junior creditors and even shareholders may get something to reach a deal. **Structural subordination**: holding-company debt ranks behind the subsidiaries' own debt for the subsidiaries' assets.

## Securitization: benefits and parties
@id securitization
@r 67
@aka securitization; asset backed securities; ABS; special purpose entity; SPE; SPV; special purpose vehicle; bankruptcy remote; originator; servicer; trustee; true sale; tranches
@see credit-enhancement; abs-types; mbs

Pooling loans or receivables (mortgages, auto loans, credit-card debt) and selling them to a **special purpose entity (SPE)** that issues securities backed by the pool's cash flows.

Parties: the **originator/seller** (makes the loans), the **SPE** (a bankruptcy-remote legal entity that buys them in a true sale), the **servicer** (collects payments), the **trustee**, and investors. Because the SPE is separate, investors depend on the assets' cash flows, not the originator's credit.

**Benefits**: lenders free up capital and can make more loans; investors get access to diversified pools with tailored risk; markets become more liquid and efficient. **Risks**: complexity, misaligned incentives (originate-to-distribute), and the 2008 crisis showed how poor underwriting can spread.

## Credit enhancement
@id credit-enhancement
@r 68
@aka internal credit enhancement; external credit enhancement; subordination; waterfall; overcollateralization; excess spread; reserve accounts; tranching; senior mezzanine equity tranche; guarantees; letter of credit
@see securitization; abs-types

Ways to protect the senior investors in a securitization:

- **Internal**: **subordination** (junior tranches absorb losses first, a "waterfall"), **overcollateralization** (collateral worth more than the securities), **excess spread** (interest on the pool above the coupons paid, held to cover losses), and reserve accounts.
- **External**: third-party guarantees, letters of credit, surety bonds (exposes investors to the guarantor's credit).

## Covered bonds
@id covered-bonds
@r 68
@aka covered bond; cover pool; dual recourse; Pfandbrief; dynamic cover pool
@see securitization

Bonds issued by a bank and backed by a segregated pool of assets (usually mortgages or public-sector loans) that stays on the bank's balance sheet. Investors have **dual recourse**: to the bank and to the cover pool. The pool is **dynamic** (the bank must replace non-performing assets). Lower risk and yield than comparable ABS.

## Non-mortgage ABS and CDOs
@id abs-types
@r 68
@aka auto loan ABS; credit card ABS; student loan ABS; revolving structure; lockout period; amortizing ABS; CDO; collateralized debt obligation; CLO; collateralized loan obligation
@see securitization; credit-enhancement

- **Auto loan ABS**: amortizing loans; prepayments; credit enhancement through subordination and reserve accounts.
- **Credit card ABS**: non-amortizing receivables; a **revolving (lockout) period** during which principal repaid is reinvested in new receivables, followed by amortization; early amortization triggers protect investors.
- **CDOs**: securities backed by a pool of debt (bonds, loans, other ABS) managed by a collateral manager. **CLOs** (backed by leveraged loans) are the main type today. Tranches get paid in order; the equity tranche gets the residual. Return depends on the manager earning more on collateral than the funding cost of the tranches.

## Mortgage-backed securities (MBS)
@id mbs
@r 69
@aka MBS; residential mortgage backed securities; RMBS; pass through securities; agency MBS; non agency MBS; Ginnie Mae; Fannie Mae; Freddie Mac; CMO; collateralized mortgage obligation; CMBS
@see prepayment-risk; securitization; cmo

- **Mortgage pass-throughs**: investors receive a pro-rata share of the pool's interest and principal (after servicing and guarantee fees). **Agency** MBS are guaranteed by government agencies or GSEs; **non-agency** MBS rely on credit enhancement.
- **Collateralized mortgage obligations (CMOs)**: redistribute pass-through cash flows into tranches with different exposures to prepayment risk.
- **Commercial MBS (CMBS)**: backed by commercial property loans; key metrics are **debt service coverage** and **loan-to-value**. Loans usually have prepayment protection (lockouts, defeasance, prepayment penalties) and **balloon risk** (borrower can't refinance at maturity).

Residential mortgage features: amortization, fixed vs adjustable rate, prepayment penalties, **recourse** vs **non-recourse** (lender can claim only the house), loan-to-value ratio.

## Prepayment risk: contraction and extension
@id prepayment-risk
@r 69
@aka prepayment risk; contraction risk; extension risk; refinancing; prepayment speed; PSA; CPR; conditional prepayment rate; SMM; weighted average life
@see mbs; cmo; negative-convexity

Homeowners can repay mortgages early, so MBS cash flows are uncertain.

- **Contraction risk**: rates fall → refinancing speeds up → principal comes back early, to be reinvested at lower rates; price gains are limited (negative convexity).
- **Extension risk**: rates rise → prepayments slow → the security lasts longer just when its yield is below market.

Prepayment speed is measured by the **conditional prepayment rate (CPR)**, an annualized rate, or the single monthly mortality rate (SMM), often relative to a **PSA** benchmark. **Weighted average life** measures the expected average time to principal repayment.

## Collateralized mortgage obligations (CMOs) and tranching
@id cmo
@r 69
@aka CMO; sequential pay tranches; PAC tranche; planned amortization class; support tranche; companion tranche; time tranching; floater tranche; IO PO
@see mbs; prepayment-risk

**Time tranching** reallocates prepayment risk:

- **Sequential-pay**: tranche A gets all principal until retired, then B, and so on. Short tranches have less extension risk; long ones less contraction risk.
- **PAC (planned amortization class)** tranches receive principal on a schedule as long as prepayments stay within a band; **support (companion)** tranches absorb the variation, so they have much more prepayment risk.
- Floating-rate tranches, and interest-only / principal-only strips, are also created.

Tranching doesn't remove prepayment risk; it moves it between investors.
