# Portfolio Management
@topic pm

## Major asset classes: historical characteristics
@id asset-classes
@r 87
@aka asset classes; historical returns; stocks vs bonds; equity risk premium history; small cap premium; risk return tradeoff; asset class correlation
@see portfolio-variance; asset-allocation

Over long histories, asset classes line up roughly by risk: Treasury bills (lowest return and volatility) < government bonds < corporate bonds < large-cap equities < small-cap equities. Higher average returns come with higher volatility. Real returns, after inflation, are what matter for building wealth. Returns of risky assets are usually **not normal**: negative skew and fat tails. Correlations between asset classes are below 1 and change over time, which is the basis for diversification.

## Risk aversion
@id risk-aversion
@r 87
@aka risk averse; risk neutral; risk seeking; risk tolerance; utility; certainty equivalent; prefer less risk
@see optimal-portfolio; risk-tolerance

A **risk-averse** investor, given two investments with the same expected return, chooses the less risky one; they need a premium to take risk. A **risk-neutral** investor cares only about expected return. A **risk-seeking** investor prefers more risk for the same return. Standard finance assumes investors are risk averse; the degree is measured by A in the utility function \(U = E(R) - \frac{1}{2}A\sigma^2\).

## Systematic and nonsystematic risk
@id systematic-risk
@r 88
@aka systematic risk; market risk; non diversifiable risk; nonsystematic risk; unsystematic risk; idiosyncratic risk; firm specific risk; diversifiable risk; total risk
@see diversification; beta; capm

**Total risk = systematic + nonsystematic.**

- **Systematic (market) risk**: affects all assets (recessions, rates, inflation); can't be diversified away; measured by [[beta]].
- **Nonsystematic (firm-specific) risk**: a CEO resigns, a factory burns; can be eliminated by diversification.

Since nonsystematic risk can be removed at almost no cost, the market only rewards **systematic** risk with higher expected returns. That is the core idea of the [[capm]].

## Return-generating models
@id return-generating-models
@r 88
@aka return generating model; factor model; macroeconomic factor model; fundamental factor model; statistical factor model; single index model
@see market-model; apt; multifactor-models

Models that estimate expected returns from one or more factors:

$$E(R_i) - R_f = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{ij}\,E(F_j)$$

- **Macroeconomic** factors: surprises in GDP, inflation, interest rates.
- **Fundamental** factors: size, value (P/B), momentum, profitability.
- **Statistical** factors: derived from return data (e.g. principal components).

The [[market-model]] is the single-factor special case.

## Sharpe ratio
@id sharpe-ratio
@r 88
@aka Sharpe ratio; reward to variability; excess return per unit of total risk; risk adjusted return
@see treynor-ratio; m-squared; capital-allocation-line; sortino-ratio

$$Sharpe = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}$$

Excess return per unit of **total** risk; the slope of the capital allocation line. Use it for an investor's entire portfolio (where total risk matters). It's unaffected by leverage (mixing with the risk-free asset), so it can rank portfolios but the number itself is hard to interpret; [[m-squared]] puts it in return terms. It assumes roughly normal returns; for skewed strategies use [[sortino-ratio]].

## Treynor ratio
@id treynor-ratio
@r 88
@aka Treynor ratio; excess return per unit of systematic risk; reward to volatility
@see sharpe-ratio; jensens-alpha; beta

$$Treynor = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\beta_p}$$

Excess return per unit of **systematic** risk. Appropriate for a portfolio that is a part of a larger, well-diversified portfolio, where only beta matters.

## M-squared (M²)
@id m-squared
@r 88
@aka M squared; M2; Modigliani; risk adjusted performance in percent; leveraged to market risk
@see sharpe-ratio

Lever or de-lever the portfolio with the risk-free asset until its volatility equals the market's, then compare returns:

$$M^2 = (R_p - R_f)\frac{\sigma_m}{\sigma_p} - (R_m - R_f)$$

It ranks portfolios exactly like the Sharpe ratio but expresses the difference in **percentage return**, which is easier to explain to clients.

## Jensen's alpha
@id jensens-alpha
@r 88, 10
@aka Jensen's alpha; alpha; abnormal return; excess return over CAPM; positive alpha; manager skill
@see capm; treynor-ratio; beta-regression

The return earned above what CAPM predicts for the portfolio's beta:

$$\alpha_p = R_p - [R_f + \beta_p(R_m - R_f)]$$

Positive alpha: the manager beat the risk-adjusted expectation (the portfolio plots above the SML). Like Treynor, it uses systematic risk only.

## Sortino ratio
@id sortino-ratio
@r 81, 88
@aka Sortino ratio; downside risk adjusted return; target downside deviation; minimum acceptable return
@see semi-deviation; sharpe-ratio

$$Sortino = \frac{R_p - MAR}{\text{downside deviation}}$$

Like the Sharpe ratio but penalizes only downside volatility below a minimum acceptable return (MAR). Better for skewed return distributions such as hedge funds and option strategies.

## Information ratio
@id information-ratio
@r 88, 3
@aka information ratio; IR; active return per unit of active risk; tracking error ratio
@see tracking-error; benchmark

$$IR = \frac{R_p - R_b}{\text{tracking error}}$$

Active return per unit of active risk: consistency of outperformance against a benchmark. Used to judge active managers.

## Security market line and CAPM applications
@id sml
@r 88, 50
@aka security market line; SML; SML vs CML; undervalued overvalued security; required return line; CAPM applications
@see capm; capital-allocation-line; jensens-alpha

The SML plots expected return against **beta**; it applies to every asset and portfolio. The CML plots expected return against **total risk (σ)** and applies only to efficient portfolios.

Applications: estimate the **cost of equity** and project **hurdle rates**; evaluate performance (alpha, Treynor); **security selection**: if a security's forecast return is above the SML, it is undervalued (buy); below it, overvalued (sell).

## Portfolio approach to investing
@id portfolio-approach
@r 89
@aka portfolio approach; diversification ratio; why diversify; think in portfolios
@see diversification; portfolio-management-process

Evaluating investments by their contribution to the whole portfolio, not one by one. A portfolio can have the same expected return as its parts with less risk. The **diversification ratio** (portfolio risk / average risk of the securities held equally) shows the benefit. Diversification protects against single-asset losses but **not** against market-wide crashes, when correlations rise.

## Defined contribution vs. defined benefit pension plans
@id db-vs-dc
@r 89, 34
@aka defined benefit; defined contribution; DB plan; DC plan; who bears investment risk; 401k; pension plan types; portability
@see pensions; investor-types

- **Defined benefit**: the employer promises a benefit based on salary and years of service; the **employer bears investment risk** and must fund any shortfall. Long horizon, liability-driven investing.
- **Defined contribution**: the employer (and employee) contribute set amounts; the **employee bears investment risk** and makes the investment choices; benefits are portable between jobs.

## Asset management industry
@id asset-management-industry
@r 89
@aka asset management industry; active vs passive management; buy side; sell side; fee compression; ETFs growth; traditional vs alternative managers; robo advisors
@see pooled-investments

The **buy side** (asset managers, pensions, insurers) invests; the **sell side** (brokers, investment banks) provides trading, research and issuance. Trends: the shift from **active to passive** (index funds and ETFs), fee pressure, consolidation, **big data and AI** in investing, **robo-advisers**, and growth of private markets and ESG. Managers can be **traditional** (stocks and bonds) or **alternative** (hedge funds, private equity, real assets).

## Mutual funds and other pooled products
@id pooled-investments
@r 89
@aka mutual fund; open end fund; closed end fund; ETF; exchange traded fund; NAV; net asset value; separately managed account; index fund; money market fund; load fund; no load fund
@see asset-management-industry; hedge-fund-vehicles

- **Open-end mutual funds**: buy and sell shares from the fund at **NAV** each day; new money creates new shares.
- **Closed-end funds**: a fixed number of shares traded on an exchange, often at a premium or discount to NAV.
- **ETFs**: trade on exchanges throughout the day; an **authorized participant** creation/redemption mechanism keeps prices near NAV; usually low cost and tax-efficient.
- **Separately managed accounts**: the investor owns the securities directly; customizable.
- **Hedge funds, private equity funds**: limited-partnership structures for qualified investors.

Fund types: money market, bond, equity, hybrid/balanced; active or index.

## Investment policy statement (IPS)
@id ips
@r 90
@aka investment policy statement; IPS; written investment plan; objectives and constraints; RRTTLLU; components of IPS
@see return-objectives; risk-tolerance; investment-constraints

A written agreement between client and adviser that sets out the client's goals, constraints and how the portfolio will be managed. It focuses the client on long-term goals, guards against emotional decisions, provides continuity if advisers change, and is the standard for judging performance.

Typical components: introduction and statement of purpose; duties and responsibilities; procedures for updating; **investment objectives** (return and risk); **constraints** (liquidity, time horizon, taxes, legal and regulatory, unique circumstances); investment guidelines (permitted assets, leverage); evaluation and review; appendices (strategic asset allocation, rebalancing policy).

## Return objectives
@id return-objectives
@r 90
@aka return objective; required return; desired return; absolute return objective; relative return objective; spending needs
@see ips; risk-tolerance

Return objectives can be **absolute** (e.g. 7% a year, or inflation + 3%) or **relative** (beat a benchmark, or a liability-based target). Distinguish the **required** return (needed to meet goals) from the **desired** return. The return objective must be consistent with the client's risk tolerance; if it isn't, the client must lower expectations, save more or accept more risk.

## Risk tolerance: willingness vs. ability
@id risk-tolerance
@r 90, 92
@aka risk tolerance; ability to take risk; willingness to take risk; risk capacity; risk attitude; risk profile; investor questionnaire
@see ips; risk-aversion

Risk tolerance combines:

- **Ability (capacity)**: objective; depends on wealth relative to needs, time horizon, income stability, and how important the goal is.
- **Willingness**: subjective; the client's psychological attitude, from questionnaires and behaviour.

When they conflict, the more conservative usually prevails. If willingness is above ability, use ability. If ability exceeds willingness, the adviser can educate the client, but should not force more risk on them.

## Investment constraints
@id investment-constraints
@r 90
@aka liquidity constraint; time horizon constraint; tax constraint; legal and regulatory constraints; unique circumstances; ESG restrictions
@see ips

- **Liquidity**: need for cash (emergencies, planned spending) and the illiquidity of current holdings.
- **Time horizon**: longer horizons generally allow more risk; multi-stage horizons (working years, retirement).
- **Taxes**: tax-exempt vs taxable accounts; which assets to hold where.
- **Legal and regulatory**: rules for pensions, insurers, trusts; insider restrictions on executives.
- **Unique circumstances**: religious or ethical preferences, ESG exclusions, concentrated holdings, special family needs.

## Asset allocation and portfolio construction
@id asset-allocation
@r 90
@aka strategic asset allocation; SAA; tactical asset allocation; TAA; asset class specification; rebalancing; core satellite; security selection; risk budgeting
@see ips; efficient-frontier; asset-classes

**Strategic asset allocation**: the long-term target weights for each asset class, set from the IPS and capital market expectations. It explains most of the variation in a portfolio's returns over time.

Asset classes should be **internally homogeneous** (similar assets) and **mutually exclusive**, with low correlations between classes, and together cover most investable assets.

Construction: **tactical asset allocation** (short-term deviations to exploit views), **security selection** within classes, **rebalancing** policy (calendar or corridor ranges), and approaches like **core-satellite** (a passive core plus active satellites) and risk budgeting.

## ESG integration in portfolios
@id esg-investing
@r 90, 21
@aka ESG investing; negative screening; exclusionary screening; positive screening; best in class; thematic investing; impact investing; ESG integration; active ownership; stewardship; engagement; sustainable investing
@see esg-factors; ips

Ways to include ESG considerations:

- **Negative (exclusionary) screening**: exclude sectors or companies (tobacco, weapons, coal).
- **Positive screening / best-in-class**: favour firms with better ESG practices relative to peers.
- **ESG integration**: include material ESG factors in analysis and valuation.
- **Thematic investing**: target themes like clean energy or water.
- **Impact investing**: seek measurable social or environmental impact along with return.
- **Active ownership (stewardship)**: engage with companies and vote proxies.

Screening narrows the opportunity set and can create tracking error against broad benchmarks.

## Cognitive errors vs. emotional biases
@id behavioral-biases
@r 91
@aka behavioral finance; cognitive errors; emotional biases; belief perseverance; information processing errors; behavioral biases list; investor psychology
@see belief-perseverance-biases; information-processing-biases; emotional-biases; forecasting-biases

**Cognitive errors** come from faulty reasoning, memory or information processing. They can often be corrected with education and better processes. Two groups: [[belief-perseverance-biases]] and [[information-processing-biases]].

**Emotional biases** come from feelings, impulses and intuition. They are harder to correct, so advisers often have to adapt to them. See [[emotional-biases]].

## Belief perseverance biases
@id belief-perseverance-biases
@r 91
@aka conservatism bias; confirmation bias; representativeness bias; illusion of control; hindsight bias; cognitive dissonance; base rate neglect
@see behavioral-biases

Holding on to existing beliefs:

- **Conservatism**: slow to update views with new information.
- **Confirmation**: seeking evidence that supports your view, ignoring the rest (e.g. holding a stock you like despite bad news).
- **Representativeness**: classifying by similarity to a stereotype, ignoring base rates and sample size ("this looks like the next big tech winner").
- **Illusion of control**: believing you can control outcomes you can't; leads to over-trading and concentration.
- **Hindsight**: believing past events were predictable ("I knew it all along").

## Information-processing biases
@id information-processing-biases
@r 91
@aka anchoring and adjustment; mental accounting; framing bias; availability bias; anchoring; recency
@see behavioral-biases

Processing information incorrectly:

- **Anchoring and adjustment**: sticking close to an initial number (a purchase price, last year's forecast).
- **Mental accounting**: treating money differently depending on its source or label (spending dividends but not capital), ignoring correlations between "buckets".
- **Framing**: decisions change with how a question is posed (gains vs losses).
- **Availability**: judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind (recent, vivid events).

## Emotional biases
@id emotional-biases
@r 91
@aka loss aversion; disposition effect; overconfidence; self control bias; status quo bias; endowment bias; regret aversion; herding; prospect theory
@see behavioral-biases; market-anomalies-behavioral

- **Loss aversion**: losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains feel good (prospect theory). Leads to the **disposition effect**: selling winners too early and holding losers too long.
- **Overconfidence**: overestimating one's knowledge and ability; under-diversification and excessive trading.
- **Self-control**: favouring short-term spending over long-term goals; under-saving.
- **Status quo**: doing nothing, keeping the current allocation.
- **Endowment**: valuing what you own more than you'd pay to buy it (inherited stocks).
- **Regret aversion**: avoiding decisions that might turn out badly; herding with the crowd so you are not wrong alone.

## Behavioral finance and market characteristics
@id market-anomalies-behavioral
@r 91
@aka market anomalies; bubbles and crashes; momentum effect; value vs growth anomaly; herding; behavioral explanation of anomalies; overreaction
@see emotional-biases; behavioral-biases

Biases can explain patterns that traditional finance struggles with:

- **Momentum**: recent winners keep winning for a while (herding, underreaction, then overreaction).
- **Bubbles and crashes**: overconfidence, confirmation and herding push prices away from value; loss aversion and panic deepen the crash.
- **Value vs growth**: investors extrapolate recent growth (representativeness), overpricing glamour stocks and underpricing value stocks.
- **Home bias**: familiarity leads to overweighting domestic stocks.

## Risk management definition and framework
@id risk-management
@r 92
@aka risk management; risk management framework; enterprise risk management; ERM; risk identification; risk measurement; risk monitoring; risk infrastructure; risk budgeting
@see risk-governance; risk-types; risk-tolerance

**Risk management** is the process of setting the level of risk an organization is willing to take, measuring the risk it is actually taking, and adjusting toward the target. The aim is not to minimize risk but to take the **right** risks to maximize value.

A framework includes: risk governance; identification and measurement of risks; **risk infrastructure** (people, systems, data); policies and processes; monitoring, mitigation and management; communication; and strategic analysis. A **risk budget** allocates the total acceptable risk across activities or asset classes.

## Risk governance
@id risk-governance
@r 92
@aka risk governance; board risk oversight; chief risk officer; CRO; risk committee; risk appetite; three lines of defense
@see risk-management

Top-down oversight of risk management by the board: setting the organization's **risk tolerance (appetite)**, aligning it with strategy, and making sure risk is managed across the enterprise. Good governance includes a **risk management committee**, a **chief risk officer** independent of business lines, clear accountability, and consistent reporting. It's best viewed enterprise-wide, since risks interact.

## Risk tolerance in risk management
@id org-risk-tolerance
@r 92
@aka organizational risk tolerance; risk appetite; what risks to take; risk tolerance and strategy
@see risk-governance; risk-tolerance

An organization's risk tolerance is how much loss it can and will accept. It depends on its ability to absorb losses (capital, liquidity, the stability of the business), its competitive position and what its owners and regulators expect. It should be set **before** a crisis, so decisions under stress follow the plan. Risk-taking within tolerance is fine; outside it, positions are cut or hedged.

## Types of risk
@id risk-types
@r 92
@aka financial risks; non financial risks; market risk; credit risk; liquidity risk; operational risk; settlement risk; legal risk; compliance risk; model risk; tail risk; solvency risk; mortality and longevity risk
@see risk-management; credit-risk; var

**Financial risks** (from financial markets):

- **Market risk**: prices, rates, FX, commodities.
- **Credit risk**: counterparties failing to pay.
- **Liquidity risk**: having to sell at a big discount, or being unable to fund.

**Non-financial risks** (from the organization or outside):

- **Operational**: people, systems, processes, external events (including cyber).
- **Settlement**, **legal**, **compliance/regulatory**, **accounting**, **model**, **tail risk** (extreme events more likely than assumed), **tax** and **solvency** risk.

Individuals face **mortality** (dying early, family loses income) and **longevity** (outliving savings) risks. Risks **interact**: a market shock can trigger margin calls (liquidity), defaults (credit) and operational strain at the same time.

## Measuring and modifying risk
@id risk-measures
@r 92
@aka risk measures; standard deviation; beta; duration; delta; gamma; vega; VaR; CVaR; stress testing; scenario analysis; risk mitigation; risk transfer; insurance; hedging; risk avoidance; risk prevention
@see var; risk-management

**Measures**: standard deviation, beta (equity), duration (bonds), the option "Greeks" (delta, gamma, vega, rho), credit measures (PD, LGD), [[var]] and CVaR, plus **stress tests** and **scenario analysis** for extreme but plausible events.

**Ways to modify risk**:

- **Prevent / avoid**: don't take the activity on.
- **Accept (self-insure)**: keep the risk, possibly with reserves; diversify.
- **Transfer**: insurance (pay premium for protection).
- **Shift**: derivatives that change the payoff distribution (forwards, options, swaps).

Choose based on cost and benefit.
